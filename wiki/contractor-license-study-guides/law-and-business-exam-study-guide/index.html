California Law and Business Exam: Full Study Guide

Take the free 20-question practice quiz ›

CSLB Law and Business exam outline (guide effective Dec 1, 2021; form 13E-LAW 03-2026)Not yet verified against the current edition

An independent study guide for the CSLB Law and Business examination, the exam every new California contractor license applicant takes in addition to a trade exam. It is built around the official CSLB content outline.

Read this first. This guide is a study aid, not an official CSLB document and not legal advice. Contractor law changes often: dollar thresholds, deadlines and fee amounts have been revised in recent years, and the CSLB's guide is dated December 2021. Where this guide gives a number, treat it as a study anchor and confirm the current figure in the CSLB's Contractors License Law & Reference Book, the Labor Code, the Business and Professions Code and the other official references before the exam. The exam is closed-book, so the goal is to know this material, not to look it up. Check cslb.ca.gov for current rules and fees.

1. Why this exam matters

Every new contractor license applicant must pass the Law and Business exam plus a trade exam (for example Class A, Class B, C-7 or C-10). It tests the legal and business side of running a contracting company: licensing rules, finances, employees, insurance, liens, contracts and safety. Waivers exist in some cases (for example, a qualifier already on an active license in good standing); check the CSLB's current rules.

People who have the trade skills often underestimate this exam. It is the broadest of the exams, and many questions are about specific deadlines, forms and rules, so it rewards memorization of the key numbers.

Contracts and Employment together are about 41% of the exam. Public Works is only 5%, but it is the easiest section to learn quickly because it is a short list of rules.

Question styles in the official guide

The three samples show the style:

  1. IRS deposits. What determines how often payroll deposits are made to the IRS? The amount of payroll tax liability.
  2. CSLB arbitration. Within how many days must an agreement to arbitrate be returned after the Registrar mails it? 30 calendar days.
  3. Accounting. A company buys a new car for general use by the sales staff: to which account is the depreciation charged? General and administrative (overhead) costs.

Expect specific legal and tax facts, deadlines, and accounting classification questions.

3. Official reference books and documents

The official list includes:

  • California Contractors License Law & Reference Book (Matthew Bender). This is the main legal reference; the CSLB also offers it online.
  • California Employer's Guide (EDD publication DE 44)
  • California Financial Code (the PACE financing rules)
  • California Labor Code
  • Get Licensed to Build (CSLB)
  • Maintain and Change Your License (CSLB)
  • NASCLA Contractors Guide to Business, Law and Project Management, 14th Edition
  • IRS Publication 15 (Circular E), Employer's Tax Guide
  • Public Works Manual (Department of Industrial Relations)
  • Cal/OSHA General Industry and Construction Safety Orders (Title 8)

Many of these are free online (the CSLB booklets, DE 44, IRS Publication 15, the DIR Public Works Manual and Cal/OSHA). The NASCLA guide and the Matthew Bender law book are the two to buy or borrow.

5. Section 1: Business Organization and Licensing (13%)

5.1 Company organization

  • Sole proprietorship: one owner, simple, unlimited personal liability; the owner is the qualifier
  • Partnership: general partners share liability; limited partners have limited liability
  • Corporation: a separate legal entity; owners (shareholders) have limited liability; formal requirements; an officer serves as the responsible managing officer (RMO)
  • LLC (limited liability company): limited liability with flexible management; requires a $100,000 employee/worker bond with the CSLB and meets the liability insurance rule
  • Joint venture: two or more licensed contractors on one project; each needs the right license and a joint venture license
  • Know the pros and cons: liability, taxes, ownership transfer, paperwork and cost

5.2 Licensing requirements

  • Who must be licensed: anyone who builds, alters, repairs or improves property, including subcontractors, when the work is valued above the statutory threshold
  • Qualifier: the RMO (owner or officer) or RME (employee who works at least 32 hours a week) who meets the experience and exam requirements and is responsible for supervising the work
  • If the qualifier leaves, the company must replace the qualifier within 90 days or the license is suspended
  • Contractor bond: $25,000, protects consumers
  • Renewal every 2 years; know active, inactive and expired status; a license can be inactive but not used for work
  • Classifications: A (general engineering), B (general building), C (specialty) and B-2 (residential remodeling); know the scope and limits
  • Change reporting: report changes in name, address, ownership, officers and qualifiers to the CSLB (generally within 90 days)
  • Workers' compensation coverage or an exemption must be on file or the license is suspended
  • Exemptions: owner-builders on their own property, certain minor work, some public utility and government work; know the owner-builder rules, including the presumption when property is sold within a year
  • Unlicensed contracting: an unlicensed person cannot sue to collect payment, may have to return payments, and faces fines and possible jail; customers can recover compensation paid
  • Discipline: the CSLB can suspend or revoke a license, fine, order restitution, and require training for violations
  • Complaints and arbitration: the CSLB investigates complaints and offers arbitration for smaller disputes; the contractor must return the agreement to arbitrate within 30 calendar days of the Registrar's mailing
  • Statutes of limitation for construction defects: patent (visible) defects 4 years; latent (hidden) defects 10 years

5.3 Advertising and subcontracting

  • Advertising: every ad, business card, bid, contract and commercial vehicle must show the contractor's license number; an advertiser must be licensed and use the name on the license; a licensed contractor cannot advertise outside their classification
  • Signs, flyers, websites, social media and online ads count as advertising
  • Subcontracting: a contractor may only hire licensed subcontractors for work needing a license; check that the sub's license is active, in the right classification, and that the sub has workers' compensation
  • Public works sub listing: on public bids, a bidder lists every subcontractor performing more than one half of one percent of the bid (Subletting and Subcontracting Fair Practices Act)
  • Bid shopping and bid peddling are prohibited on public works
  • A contractor is responsible for work done by subs and for the sub's compliance, including safety, wages and insurance

6. Section 2: Business Finances (15%)

6.1 Cash management

  • Cash flow is the movement of money in and out; profit does not equal cash; many contractors fail from cash shortages despite profitable jobs
  • Sources of cash problems: slow payment by owners, retention, front-loading costs, unbilled work, too many jobs at once, poor collections
  • Improve cash flow: bill promptly and accurately, front-load the schedule of values within the legal limits, collect on time, negotiate payment terms, limit retention, maintain a line of credit
  • Working capital = current assets − current liabilities
  • Keep a cash reserve; forecast cash weekly or monthly

6.2 Budget and planning

  • Direct (job) costs: labor, materials, equipment and subcontractors on a specific job
  • Indirect costs (overhead): costs of running the company that are not charged to a job. The official sample question shows that depreciation of a company car used by sales staff belongs in general and administrative (overhead) costs
  • Typical overhead: office rent, office staff, insurance, licenses, vehicles used for management, marketing, accounting and legal, utilities
  • Overhead rate = annual overhead ÷ annual direct costs (or revenue); recover it in every bid
  • Markup and margin: price = cost × (1 + markup); margin = profit ÷ price; a 25% markup is a 20% margin
  • Break-even is where revenue equals total cost
  • Budget by job, by month and by year; compare actual to budget; use job costing with cost codes

6.3 Taxes

  • Federal and state income tax depending on the form of business
  • Payroll taxes (employer): Social Security 6.2% and Medicare 1.45% (together 7.65% employer share, matched by the employee); federal unemployment (FUTA); California unemployment insurance (UI) and Employment Training Tax (ETT); employees pay State Disability Insurance (SDI)
  • Withholding: employers withhold federal and state income tax and the employee share of Social Security, Medicare and SDI
  • Deposit schedules (federal): monthly or semiweekly depending on the tax liability in the lookback period (the official sample question: the amount of the payroll liability); a $100,000 liability requires next-day deposit
  • Forms: W-4 and DE 4 (employee withholding), I-9 (work eligibility), W-2 (annual wages), 1099 (independent contractors), 941 (quarterly federal), 940 (annual FUTA), DE 9 and DE 9C (quarterly California)
  • Sales and use tax: rules on materials depend on the contract type (lump-sum, time and materials); the CDTFA administers it
  • Independent contractor or employee: California uses the ABC test; there are exemptions for licensed subcontractors in construction in some cases; misclassification brings penalties
  • Record keeping: payroll records at least 3 years; tax records at least 4 years

6.4 Financial reporting

  • Balance sheet: assets = liabilities + owner's equity; a snapshot at one date
  • Income statement (profit and loss): revenue − expenses = net income, over a period
  • Cash flow statement: operating, investing and financing cash movements
  • Ratios:
  • Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities (a measure of short-term health)
  • Gross profit margin = (revenue − direct costs) ÷ revenue
  • Debt-to-equity = total liabilities ÷ owner's equity
  • Work in progress (WIP) schedule: compares costs and billings on jobs; overbilling (billed more than earned) and underbilling (earned more than billed) affect cash and risk
  • Accounting methods: cash basis (record when cash moves) versus accrual (record when earned or incurred); percentage-of-completion for long jobs
  • Bonding and credit: sureties and lenders look at financial statements, working capital and net worth

7. Section 3: Employment Requirements (20%)

7.1 Hiring employees

  • New hires complete the I-9 within 3 business days, W-4 and DE 4, and a wage notice (Labor Code section 2810.5)
  • Report new hires to the EDD within 20 days
  • Minimum wage is set by the state and by some cities; check the current figure
  • Exempt vs non-exempt: exempt employees must meet the duties test and a minimum salary (twice the state minimum wage for full-time); non-exempt employees receive overtime
  • Independent contractor vs employee: apply the ABC test; misclassification is costly
  • Discrimination and harassment: the California Fair Employment and Housing Act applies to employers with 5 or more employees; employers must provide harassment prevention training (2 hours for supervisors, 1 hour for others) every 2 years
  • Apprentices in apprenticeable trades must be registered with the Division of Apprenticeship Standards on public works

7.2 Supervising and training employees

  • A supervisor must understand the work, safety rules and the law; train employees for the tasks and hazards they face
  • Cal/OSHA training: injury and illness prevention, hazard communication, heat illness, equipment, fall protection and specific hazards
  • Safety meetings: at least every 10 working days for crew leaders and supervisors, and tailgate meetings
  • Use clear instructions, document training and sign-in sheets, and correct unsafe behavior
  • Keep required postings in the workplace: wage orders, minimum wage, workers' compensation, safety and health, harassment, and others

7.3 Evaluation and record keeping

  • Evaluate performance regularly and fairly; document in writing; discipline and termination should be consistent and documented
  • At-will employment has limits: no termination for discrimination, retaliation, whistleblowing, protected leave or workers' compensation claims
  • Records: payroll records at least 3 years; personnel files; I-9s (3 years after hire or 1 year after termination, whichever is later); injury logs for 5 years; tax records 4 years
  • Employees can inspect personnel and payroll records on request

7.4 Payroll

  • Overtime: 1.5 times the regular rate for hours over 8 in a day, over 40 in a week, and the first 8 hours on the seventh consecutive day; double time for hours over 12 in a day and over 8 on the seventh day
  • Meal and rest breaks: 30-minute unpaid meal break before the end of the 5th hour (a second for shifts over 10 hours); 10-minute paid rest break for every 4 hours or major fraction
  • Pay frequency: at least twice a month; final pay is due immediately on discharge, and within 72 hours if the employee quits without notice (immediately if given 72 hours' notice)
  • Wage statements (pay stubs) must show hours, rates, deductions and the employer
  • Paid sick leave: at least 5 days or 40 hours a year
  • Deductions: federal and state income tax, Social Security, Medicare, SDI; garnishments follow court orders
  • Prevailing wage applies on public works (see section 10)
  • Penalties apply for late payment, unpaid overtime and misclassified workers

8. Section 4: Insurance and Liens (12%)

8.1 Workers' compensation insurance

  • Required for every employer with one or more employees, including part-time and family workers; a contractor license is suspended automatically if coverage lapses
  • A sole proprietor or the officer of a corporation with no employees may file an exemption; an exemption does not cover any employee
  • Premium = payroll × a classification rate, adjusted by the experience modification rate; premiums are audited
  • Uninsured subcontractors: a contractor can be liable for injuries to their employees; always collect certificates of insurance
  • Benefits: medical care, temporary and permanent disability, death benefits; the employee is generally limited to workers' compensation

8.2 Business insurance

  • General liability: covers injury and property damage to third parties
  • Commercial auto: vehicles used for business
  • Builder's risk: covers the project under construction
  • Umbrella and excess: additional liability limits
  • Equipment (inland marine): tools and machinery
  • Professional liability for design or advice, and pollution coverage where needed
  • Bonds are not insurance: a bid bond guarantees the bidder will sign the contract; a performance bond guarantees completion; a payment bond guarantees payment of subs and suppliers; a license bond protects the public
  • Certificates of insurance name the insured, limits and policy dates; additional insured endorsements extend coverage to the owner
  • Occurrence policies cover events during the policy period; claims-made policies cover claims made during the period

8.3 Liens and other remedies

Mechanics lien (private projects):

  • A claim against the property for unpaid labor, services or materials
  • Preliminary notice (20-day notice): subcontractors and suppliers must serve it within 20 days of first furnishing to preserve full lien rights; the direct contractor does not need one
  • Recording deadlines:
  • If the owner records a notice of completion: original contractors have 60 days; subcontractors and suppliers have 30 days
  • If no notice of completion: 90 days after completion for all claimants
  • Foreclose within 90 days after recording the lien, or it expires
  • Release forms: the law provides statutory forms for conditional and unconditional waivers and releases for progress and final payments; use the exact statutory form; an unconditional release is effective when signed, regardless of payment
  • Notice to owner (the mechanics lien warning) must be in home improvement contracts

Public works: no mechanics lien on public property; use a stop payment notice and a claim on the payment bond. Preliminary notice is still required.

Other remedies: negotiation, mediation, arbitration (including the CSLB's program), small claims court (up to a limited dollar amount), civil suit, and complaints. Prompt payment laws set deadlines: private owners must pay progress payments within 30 days of an undisputed invoice; contractors must pay subs within 7 days after receiving payment; public agencies pay undisputed progress payments within 30 days.

9. Section 5: Contract Requirements and Execution (21%)

9.1 Bidding

  • Review plans and specs; do a takeoff; price labor, materials, equipment and subs; add overhead, profit and contingency
  • Direct cost plus overhead plus profit equals the bid price
  • Bid documents: invitation, instructions, bid form, bonds, addenda; follow the instructions exactly; a non-responsive bid is rejected
  • Bid bond (often 10%) guarantees the winning bidder will enter the contract
  • Bid errors: on public works, a bidder may seek relief from a clerical error if notice is given within a short period (5 working days) after the bid opening; know that the right is limited
  • Public works award: to the lowest responsible and responsive bidder
  • Bid shopping (using one sub's price to pressure another) is prohibited on public works

9.2 Cost control

  • Job cost reports compare actual to estimated cost by cost code; review weekly
  • Track labor hours, material, equipment, sub costs and change orders; watch productivity
  • Schedule of values breaks the contract into billable parts; use it for progress billing
  • Control waste, theft and rework; use purchase orders; match invoices to deliveries
  • Forecast the cost to complete and the final profit; act on overruns early

9.3 Project organization

  • Schedules: bar charts and critical path method; update regularly; the critical path has zero float
  • Documentation: daily logs, photos, meeting minutes, submittals, RFIs, change orders, correspondence, inspection reports
  • Submittals are reviewed before ordering; RFIs resolve unclear plans
  • Coordinate subs, deliveries and inspections; hold regular meetings
  • Closeout: punch list, as-built drawings, warranties, manuals, final inspections, final payment and lien releases

9.4 Contracts

Elements of a valid contract: offer, acceptance, consideration, capable parties, lawful purpose.

Contract types: lump sum (fixed price), unit price, cost plus a fee, time and materials, guaranteed maximum price, design-build. Know who bears the risk in each.

Home improvement contracts (work on an owner's residence) have the strictest rules:

  • Must be in writing above the statutory threshold, signed by both parties, with the owner receiving a copy
  • Must include: the contractor's name, address and license number; the owner's name; a description of the work and materials; the contract price; a start date and substantial completion date; a payment schedule; the right to cancel, the mechanics lien warning, CSLB information, and the arbitration disclosure where used
  • Down payment: the lesser of 10% of the contract price or $1,000
  • Progress payments may not exceed the value of work completed and materials delivered
  • Right to cancel within 3 business days after signing (the contract includes a notice of cancellation form)
  • Change orders must be in writing and signed by both parties, stating the scope, price and time change
  • Retention on residential and other private work is limited to 5%
  • Warranty and right to repair laws apply to residential construction; defect claims have 4 year (patent) and 10 year (latent) limits

Other clauses: scope, price, payment terms, time, liquidated damages, indemnity, insurance, warranties, termination, dispute resolution, change orders, and flow-down to subs.

9.5 Payments

  • Progress payments are billed against the schedule of values with retention held
  • Retention is capped at 5%; on public works it must be released within 60 days of completion
  • Final payment follows completion, acceptance and lien releases
  • Conditional waivers go with the payment request; unconditional waivers are signed after payment is received
  • Subs must be paid within 7 days of the contractor receiving payment; withholding is limited to disputed amounts
  • Mediation and arbitration can resolve disputes; keep records

10. Section 6: Public Works (5%)

  • Prevailing wage: required on public works projects over $1,000; the Department of Industrial Relations sets the basic hourly rate plus fringe benefits by craft, county and time; pay at least the prevailing rate for each worker's classification
  • Certified payroll records: keep weekly records of each worker's classification, hours and pay; submit them electronically as the law requires; keep them at least 3 years and make them available
  • DIR registration: contractors and subs must be registered with the DIR (annual fee) before bidding or working on a public works project
  • Overtime and hours: over 8 hours a day and 40 a week is overtime; penalties for violations
  • Apprentices: employ apprentices in apprenticeable crafts at a ratio of 1 apprentice hour per 5 journeyman hours; submit award information to the apprenticeship committees
  • Penalties: an underpaid worker can bring back wages plus penalties of up to $200 per worker per day
  • Responsibility: a prime contractor is responsible for wage violations by its subs
  • Bonds: bid bond (usually 10%), performance bond (usually 100%) and payment bond (100% for contracts over $25,000)
  • Insurance: workers' compensation, general liability and other coverage per the contract; certificates must be filed before work
  • Retention is capped at 5% and released within 60 days after completion
  • Subcontractor listing: list subs over one half of one percent of the bid
  • Prompt payment: public agencies pay undisputed progress payments within 30 days
  • Stop payment notices and bond claims replace liens on public property

11. Section 7: Safety (14%)

11.1 Training and reporting requirements

  • Injury and Illness Prevention Program (IIPP): every California employer must have a written program with these elements: responsibility, compliance, communication, hazard assessment, accident investigation, hazard correction, training and record keeping
  • Reporting serious injuries: report a work-related death, serious injury or illness (such as a hospitalization over 24 hours, amputation or loss of an eye) to Cal/OSHA immediately, within 8 hours at most
  • Injury records: keep the OSHA 300 log, 300A summary and 301 incident report; post the 300A summary each year from February 1 to April 30; keep records 5 years
  • Workers' compensation claims: give the injured employee a claim form (DWC-1) within one working day of learning of the injury; report to the insurer promptly
  • Safety meetings: at least every 10 working days for crew leaders, plus tailgate meetings
  • Heat illness prevention: water, shade, rest breaks and training for outdoor work
  • Hazard communication: a written program, labels, safety data sheets (SDS) and training
  • Postings: Cal/OSHA notices, the 300A summary and others

11.2 General safety

  • Follow the Construction Safety Orders for fall protection, scaffolds, ladders, excavations, trenching, electrical safety, machinery, cranes and personal protective equipment
  • Excavations of 5 feet or more need a protective system; a permit is required for certain deep excavations and trenches; a competent person inspects daily
  • Lockout/tagout for machinery; guarding; proper tools and equipment
  • Personal protective equipment (hard hats, eye and ear protection, gloves, footwear, respirators, high-visibility clothing) must be provided and used
  • Fire protection, housekeeping, first aid supplies and trained first aid responders
  • Emergency plans and clear communication

11.3 Hazardous and unknown materials

  • Treat unknown materials as hazardous until identified; stop work and have them tested
  • Asbestos: many materials in older buildings contain it; contractors working with more than a small amount must be certified and register with Cal/OSHA; follow notification, containment and disposal rules
  • Lead: lead-based paint in older buildings; follow lead-safe work practices
  • Silica dust from cutting concrete and masonry; use wet methods, ventilation and respirators
  • Hazardous waste: use proper containers, labeling, storage and disposal with a licensed hauler and manifest; do not dump on site or in drains
  • Underground tanks, contaminated soil and mold need specialists and notification
  • Proposition 65 requires warnings for exposure to listed chemicals

12. Calculation practice (answers at the bottom)

  1. A $240,000 contract has 5% retention. At a progress payment of $60,000, how much is paid and how much is held?
  2. A home improvement contract is $7,500. What is the maximum down payment?
  3. A home improvement contract is $24,000. What is the maximum down payment?
  4. A worker earns $28 an hour and works 10 hours a day for 5 days. What is the gross weekly pay (daily overtime only)?
  5. A company's annual overhead is $150,000 and annual direct costs are $750,000. What is the overhead rate on direct costs?
  6. Direct cost is $400,000, overhead is 15% of direct cost, and profit is 10% of direct cost plus overhead. What is the bid price?
  7. A company has current assets of $320,000 and current liabilities of $200,000. What are the working capital and the current ratio?
  8. A notice of completion is recorded on March 1. What is the last day for an original contractor to record a lien (60 days), and for a subcontractor (30 days)?
  9. A contractor receives a progress payment on June 3. By what date must the subcontractor be paid under the 7-day rule?
  10. An employer's payroll tax liability in the lookback period is $30,000. Under the federal rules, how often are deposits generally made?

Answers: (1) Retention 5% × $60,000 = $3,000 held; $57,000 paid. (2) 10% = $750, which is less than $1,000, so $750. (3) 10% = $2,400, which is more than $1,000, so $1,000. (4) Regular 8 × 5 = 40 hours × $28 = $1,120; overtime 2 × 5 = 10 hours × $42 = $420; total $1,540. (5) 150,000 ÷ 750,000 = 20%. (6) 400,000 × 1.15 = $460,000; × 1.10 = $506,000. (7) Working capital $120,000; current ratio 1.6. (8) Original contractor: April 30 (60 days after March 1); subcontractor: March 31 (30 days). (9) June 10. (10) Monthly (the threshold for monthly deposits is a lookback liability of $50,000 or less).

13. Six-week study plan

Assumes 6 to 8 hours per week. The Law and Business exam is mostly memorization, so plan on repetition.

WeekFocusTasks
1Orientation and licensingRead the official CSLB guide, Get Licensed to Build and Maintain and Change Your License; learn business forms, qualifiers, bonds and advertising rules
2FinancesCash flow, overhead, markup vs margin, financial statements, ratios, taxes
3EmploymentHiring, wage and hour rules, overtime, breaks, payroll, taxes, the EDD guide DE 44
4Insurance and liensWorkers' compensation, business insurance, bonds, mechanics liens and deadlines, release forms
5Contracts, public works and safetyHome improvement contract rules, change orders, payments, prevailing wage, IIPP, reporting, hazardous materials
6Practice and reviewTimed practice; memorize the numbers table; review weak areas

Habits that work

  • Build a flash card deck of numbers and deadlines: 20 days, 30 days, 60 days, 90 days, 7 days, 10%/$1,000, 5%, 3 business days, 10 years. These are the points the exam loves.
  • Learn the forms and who files them: preliminary notice, notice of completion, lien, release, DWC-1, 300 log, DE 4, I-9.
  • Think like a regulator: the question often asks what a contractor must do, what is prohibited, or what the first step is.
  • Read the question twice. Look for "minimum," "maximum," "first," "not" and "BEST."
  • Answer every question. No penalty for guessing.
  • Check recent law changes (thresholds, bond amounts, fees); the exam follows the CSLB's current references.

14. Where this fits in the licensing process

  1. Meet the experience requirement for your classification and document it.
  2. Submit the CSLB application and fingerprints.
  3. Pass the Law and Business exam and the trade exam. They can be taken separately; many people take Law and Business first because the material is shared by all classifications.
  4. Post the contractor bond and pay the license fee.

See also the Class A, Class B, C-7 and C-10 study guides in this knowledge base.

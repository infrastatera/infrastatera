California Class A General Engineering Exam: Full Study Guide

CSLB General Engineering (A) exam outline, 2025 code editionsNot yet verified against the current edition

An independent study guide for the CSLB General Engineering (A) trade examination, built around the official CSLB content outline and written for people who work in public works and infrastructure.

Read this first. This guide is a study aid, not an official CSLB document. Numbers, slopes, depths and test values are the commonly used ones; project specifications, Caltrans documents and the current Cal/OSHA orders govern in practice, so confirm every value in the reference books. The exam is closed-book, so the goal is to know this material, not to look it up. Fees, rules and exam content change; check cslb.ca.gov before you apply.

1. What a Class A license is

The General Engineering (A) classification covers fixed works that need specialized engineering knowledge and skill: roads and highways, bridges, tunnels, underground utilities and pipelines, sewer and water systems, drainage, flood control, grading, dams and similar infrastructure. It is the classic prime contractor license for public works such as Caltrans and city or county projects.

Things to know:

  • A Class A contractor can bid and manage jobs that combine several trades, but specialty work (for example electrical) must be performed under the right specialty license, either the contractor's own or a subcontractor's.
  • The exam tests the whole job: planning, earthwork, piping, concrete, safety and traffic control, not one trade deeply.

Piping, Safety, Planning/Estimating and Earthwork together are about 70% of the exam. Spend your time there first.

Question styles in the official guide

The guide's sample questions show the flavor. One asks which connection is used to flush a water line (a blow-off valve). One asks why the rate of pour is supervised for high wall forms (to avoid form collapse). One asks for the approximate backfill after removing a 10,000-gallon underground tank (about 50 cubic yards). So expect practical "what would you do or use" questions plus volume math.

California code books can be viewed free online through the Building Standards Commission, and Cal/OSHA Title 8 is viewable at dir.ca.gov.

4. Math you must be able to do fast

Core formulas and conversions

  • Cubic yards = (length ft × width ft × depth ft) ÷ 27
  • 1 cubic foot = 7.48 gallons, so 1 gallon = 0.1337 ft³
  • Area of a circle = π × r²; volume of a cylinder = π × r² × length
  • Grade (%) = rise ÷ run × 100
  • Stationing: 1+00 = 100 ft; 12+50 = 1,250 ft
  • Bank, loose and compacted yards: loose yards = bank yards × (1 + swell %); compacted yards are usually fewer than bank yards because the soil is densified
  • Relative compaction (%) = field dry density ÷ lab maximum dry density × 100
  • Level survey: HI = benchmark elevation + backsight; point elevation = HI − foresight
  • Average end area: volume (yd³) = (A1 + A2) ÷ 2 × length ÷ 27
  • 1 ton of air conditioning = 12,000 BTU per hour
  • Rebar: bar number ÷ 8 = diameter in inches (#5 = 5/8 in.)
  • Rebar weight per foot: #3 0.376 lb, #4 0.668 lb, #5 1.043 lb, #6 1.502 lb, #7 2.044 lb, #8 2.670 lb
  • Taper length for traffic control (speed 45 mph or more): L = W × S (W is the lane or offset width in ft, S is speed in mph); for 40 mph or less: L = W × S² ÷ 60

Worked examples

Tank removal backfill. 10,000 gal × 0.1337 = 1,337 ft³ ÷ 27 = 49.5 yd³ (about 50). This is the official-style question answered.

Vertical shored trench. 200 ft long, 4 ft wide, 8 ft deep: 200 × 4 × 8 ÷ 27 = 237 yd³ bank. With 25% swell: 237 × 1.25 = 296 loose yards. At 12 loose yards per truck, that's about 25 loads.

Sloped trench. Type B soil, 6 ft deep, 1:1 side slopes, 3 ft bottom width, 100 ft long:

  • Top width = 3 + 6 + 6 = 15 ft; average width = 9 ft
  • Cross-section = 9 × 6 = 54 ft²; volume = 54 × 100 = 5,400 ft³ = 200 yd³

Concrete slab. 40 ft × 20 ft × 6 in. thick: 40 × 20 × 0.5 = 400 ft³ ÷ 27 = 14.8 yd³. Order with 5-10% waste: about 15.5 to 16.5 yd³.

Level survey. Benchmark 245.60, backsight 3.15, foresight 7.45: HI = 248.75; point elevation = 248.75 − 7.45 = 241.30.

Average end area. End areas of 40 ft² and 60 ft², 100 ft apart: (40 + 60) ÷ 2 × 100 ÷ 27 = 185 yd³.

Relative compaction. Lab maximum 120 pcf, field test 110.4 pcf: 110.4 ÷ 120 = 92%.

Pipe slope. Invert drops 2.5 ft over 500 ft: 2.5 ÷ 500 = 0.5%.

5. Section 1: Planning and Estimating (15%)

5.1 Reviewing plans and specifications

  • Know the contract documents: plans, standard plans, standard specifications, special provisions, addenda, geotechnical report and bid forms.
  • Order of precedence: more specific documents control over more general ones. On Caltrans work, special provisions generally control over plans, which control over the Standard Plans and Standard Specifications. Written dimensions beat scaled dimensions. When documents conflict, the contractor asks (an RFI) before pricing or building.
  • Read the legend, general notes, plan and profile sheets, typical sections, cross-sections, details, quantity tables and bid item lists.
  • Understand scale, north arrow, stationing, benchmark references and datum.

5.2 Code compliance

  • Know which code covers which work: Building Code (structures), Plumbing Code (building plumbing), Electrical Code, Standard Specifications (highway and utility work), Cal/OSHA (worker safety), and permit and inspection requirements from the agency.
  • Know common compliance items: permits, special inspections, accessibility (ADA) requirements, stormwater compliance and environmental permits.

5.3 Evaluating job sites

Walk the site before bidding:

  • Access, staging areas, haul routes, traffic, and work-hour limits
  • Existing utilities (call 811 / DigAlert), overhead lines, and utility conflicts
  • Soil, groundwater and contamination (read the geotechnical report)
  • Drainage and flooding risk, protected species, cultural resources
  • Neighbors, noise, dust, and work windows

5.4 Coordinating with other trades and agencies

  • Sequence work so each crew has access, for example utilities before paving
  • Coordinate utility owners, inspectors, subcontractors and traffic control
  • Critical path method (CPM): the critical path is the longest chain of dependent activities; activities with float can slip without delaying the job; the critical path has zero float
  • Submittals, RFIs, pre-construction meetings and weekly coordination

5.5 Estimating costs

  • Quantity takeoff from plans; add waste and shrink/swell factors
  • Direct costs: labor (use prevailing wage on public works), materials, equipment (owned or rented), subcontracts
  • Indirect costs: supervision, insurance, bonds, permits, mobilization, field office
  • Markup: overhead plus profit; contingency for risk
  • Unit-price bids and lump-sum bids; change orders and extra work
  • Public works award is to the lowest responsible, responsive bidder; bid and performance/payment bonds are normally required

6. Section 2: Structural Principles (6%)

6.1 Constructing structures

  • Load path: loads travel from roof or deck to supports, to foundation, to soil
  • Dead load (permanent weight) and live load (people, traffic, movable items); also wind, seismic, snow and earth pressure loads
  • Stress = force ÷ area; the basic types are tension, compression, shear, bending and torsion
  • Common materials: reinforced concrete (concrete is strong in compression, steel carries tension), structural steel, timber, masonry

6.2 Seismic requirements

  • California design is driven by earthquake forces; know the idea of lateral force resisting systems: shear walls, braced frames and moment frames
  • Ductile detailing: reinforcement spliced and anchored so members can deform without sudden failure
  • Hold-downs and anchor bolts tie structures to foundations; connections matter as much as members
  • Liquefaction and fault hazards are evaluated in the geotechnical report
  • Seismic joints (gaps) keep adjacent structures from pounding

6.3 Basic engineering

  • Simply supported beam with uniform load: maximum moment = w × L² ÷ 8
  • Retaining walls resist overturning, sliding and bearing failure; drainage behind them is critical
  • Spread footings, mat foundations and deep foundations spread loads to suitable soil

7. Section 3: Earthwork and Surveying (15%)

7.1 Surveying job sites

  • Benchmark (BM): a known elevation. Temporary benchmark (TBM): one set for the job.
  • Staking: offset stakes, grade stakes and cut/fill marks; centerline control and stationing
  • Instruments: level and rod, total station, GNSS (GPS); keep a field book and check closing errors
  • Contour lines: closer together means steeper ground; a closed contour shows a hill or depression
  • Cut and fill: cut is excavation below existing grade; fill is placement above it; balance the site to cut costs
  • Methods: average end area (cross-sections), grid and borrow-pit calculations

7.2 Preparing job sites

  • Clearing and grubbing, demolition, topsoil stripping and stockpiling
  • Temporary fencing, haul roads, stabilized construction entrances, dust control
  • Dewatering where groundwater is high
  • Erosion and sediment control BMPs: silt fence, fiber rolls, gravel bags, inlet protection, stabilized entrances, hydroseeding; a SWPPP (storm water pollution prevention plan) is required on projects disturbing one acre or more

7.3 Assessing soil conditions

  • Soil types: gravel and sand (granular, drain well), silt, clay (plastic, holds water, expands and shrinks)
  • Expansive clay damages slabs and pavements; it is treated by lime, moisture control or removal
  • Read the boring logs and know what groundwater depth, bearing capacity and liquid limit mean
  • OSHA soil classes for trenching:

Stable rockNatural solid mineralVertical

7.4 Compaction and expansion

  • Compaction is tested against a lab maximum density (Proctor test); typical requirements are 90% to 95% relative compaction, but the specification governs
  • Optimum moisture content: too dry will not compact, too wet will pump and shear
  • Place fill in thin lifts (often 6 to 12 in. loose), compact each lift, and test with a nuclear gauge or sand cone
  • Equipment: sheepsfoot (clay), vibratory smooth drum (granular), rubber-tire rollers, plate compactors in trenches
  • Swell and shrink: soil expands when excavated (bank to loose) and shrinks when compacted; plan haul and import quantities accordingly
  • Equipment basics: excavators, loaders, dozers, scrapers, graders, haul trucks, and what each does best

8. Section 4: Subsurface, Surface and Elevated Piping Work (21%)

This is the largest section. Learn it well.

8.1 Shoring and sloping excavations

Core rules under Cal/OSHA:

  • A protective system (sloping, benching, shoring or shield) is required for excavations 5 ft or deeper, and for shallower ones where a competent person sees a hazard
  • A competent person inspects the excavation daily, before work starts, and after rain or other changes
  • Excavations of 5 ft or more generally need an excavation/trench permit from Cal/OSHA, and those over 20 ft need protective systems designed by a registered engineer
  • Provide a ladder or exit within 25 ft of workers in trenches 4 ft deep or more
  • Keep spoil piles and equipment at least 2 ft from the edge
  • Test for hazardous atmospheres where oxygen deficiency or gas is possible
  • Keep workers out of the path of loads; use hydraulic shoring, trench shields (trench boxes) and slide rail systems properly

8.2 Utility locating and protection

  • Call 811 (DigAlert in Southern California) at least 2 working days before excavating
  • Respect the tolerance zone (24 in. on each side of a marked utility); expose utilities by hand or vacuum excavation
  • Know the marking colors: red electric, yellow gas and oil, orange communications, blue potable water, green sewer and drain, purple reclaimed water, pink survey, white proposed excavation

8.3 Preventing erosion

  • Control runoff with berms, swales and sediment basins; stabilize slopes with mulch, erosion control blankets and plantings
  • Keep sediment on site; protect storm drain inlets and watercourses

8.4 Tunneling, drilling and boring

  • Trenchless methods: jack and bore (steel casing pushed with an auger), horizontal directional drilling (HDD, for flexible pipe such as HDPE and conduit), pipe jacking and microtunneling
  • Open-cut versus trenchless: trenchless avoids surface disruption at roads, railroads and streams but needs launch and receiving pits
  • Tunneling is covered by the Cal/OSHA tunnel safety orders and a specific permit; know the idea of gassy classifications, ventilation, ground support and emergency plans

8.5 Installing pipelines

  • Pipe materials: ductile iron, PVC (C900 for water), HDPE, reinforced concrete (RCP), corrugated metal and plastic, vitrified clay, ABS, steel
  • Trench zones: bedding, haunching, pipe zone, initial backfill, final backfill; use the specified bedding material and compact it evenly under the haunches
  • Grade control: gravity sewer and storm drain are laid upgrade with bell ends upstream; check invert elevation with a laser or grade rod; keep slope per plan
  • Cover: water mains commonly have about 3 ft of cover; follow the plans and specification
  • Separation: water mains are kept at least 10 ft horizontally from sanitary sewers and 1 ft vertically above them
  • Thrust blocks or restrained joints are needed at bends, tees, dead ends and valves
  • Tracer wire and warning tape go above non-metallic pipe
  • Manholes and structures: set to line and grade, use proper joints and waterproofing; channelize flows
  • Valves: gate or butterfly (isolation), check (one-way), air release (high points), blow-off (low points and dead ends; used to flush), post indicator valve (fire service)
  • Joints: bell and spigot with gaskets, mechanical joints, fused (HDPE), flanged, restrained

8.6 Connecting and testing systems

  • Water lines: flush, disinfect (chlorine) and take bacteriological samples before use; hydrostatic pressure test, commonly 150 psi or 1.5 times working pressure, for a specified time, with leakage limits
  • Sewer and drain: low-pressure air test, water exfiltration or infiltration tests, mandrel test for deflection of flexible pipe (commonly 5% allowed), and CCTV inspection
  • Tie-ins: schedule shutdowns, use temporary bypass or hot taps as allowed, notify customers
  • Always follow the specification for test pressures and times

8.7 Dry utilities

  • Electric, gas, telephone, cable and fiber are the "dry utilities," usually in a joint trench with required separation and depth
  • Install conduit, pull boxes and vaults to the owner utility's standards; leave pull ropes
  • Respect minimum depths, clearances to other utilities and the owner's inspection requirements

9. Section 5: Foundation and Concrete Construction (11%)

9.1 Forms and falsework

  • Forms must withstand the fluid pressure of wet concrete, which rises with the rate of pour and the height. This is why the rate of pour must be supervised on tall wall forms (to avoid form failure).
  • Forms need ties, walers, braces and tight joints; coat with form release
  • Falsework is temporary support for bridges and elevated slabs; it needs a design by an engineer, inspection before the pour, and proper sequence for removal
  • Strip forms and shores only when the concrete has reached the specified strength

9.2 Reinforcing steel

  • Grade, size, spacing and clearances come from the plans
  • Cover protects steel from corrosion and fire: about 3 in. when cast against soil; 1 1/2 to 2 in. when exposed to weather, depending on bar size
  • Use chairs, bolsters and ties to hold steel in place; lap splices per plans

9.3 Placing and finishing concrete

  • Check the mix ticket: strength (f'c at 28 days), slump, air, aggregate and water-cement ratio
  • More water means weaker, more shrinkage cracking; do not add water on site beyond what is allowed
  • Place continuously and avoid long drops (segregation); consolidate with vibration, but do not over-vibrate
  • Finishing sequence: screed, bull float, wait for bleed water to leave, float and trowel or broom
  • Curing: keep moist or covered, at least 7 days for normal concrete; use curing compound or wet burlap
  • Hot weather: retarders, cool materials, shade and wind protection; cold weather: protect from freezing until it gains strength
  • Joints: control joints (cut early, about one quarter of the slab depth) to guide cracking; construction joints at planned stops; expansion joints where the concrete meets structures
  • Testing: slump test, air content, cast cylinders for lab strength tests

9.4 Piles and foundations

  • Shallow foundations: spread footings, strip footings, mat slabs
  • Deep foundations: driven piles (timber, steel H-pile, pipe, precast concrete), cast-in-drilled-hole (CIDH) piles and drilled shafts (common on highway structures), micropiles
  • Drilled shafts need casing or slurry in caving soil and a tremie to place concrete under water or slurry
  • Keep excavations for footings clean, dry and on undisturbed bearing soil, and inspect before placing steel

10. Section 6: Buildings, Structures and Related Work (9%)

10.1 Mechanical systems

  • Know the components of HVAC: equipment, ductwork, supply and return, thermostats, ventilation and exhaust
  • Ductwork should be sealed and insulated; equipment needs service access and condensate drains
  • 12,000 BTU per hour equals 1 ton of cooling

10.2 Plumbing pipes

  • Drain, waste and vent (DWV) systems: proper slope (about 1/4 in. per ft for small pipe), traps, vents and cleanouts
  • Water supply: material (copper, PEX, CPVC), backflow prevention, pressure testing and insulation
  • Test before covering: water or air test per the code

10.3 Electrical systems

  • Basic knowledge of conduit, wire, panels, grounding and GFCI protection
  • Know that electrical work must be performed under the proper electrical license and permit
  • Keep conduit protected, supported and sized per the code; maintain clearances from other utilities

10.4 Demolition, disposal and recycling

  • Before demolition, survey for asbestos and lead-based paint; abatement is done by certified contractors, with air district notification (for example SCAQMD Rule 1403 in Southern California)
  • Disconnect and cap utilities, protect adjacent property, control dust and noise
  • Shore or brace structures as they are taken down; follow a sequence from the top down
  • Sort, recycle and divert waste (CALGreen sets a construction waste diversion target, and local agencies may require more)
  • Haul hazardous waste with a manifest to a permitted facility

11. Section 7: Roadwork and Paving (5%)

11.1 Base for highways, roads and parking lots

  • Layers from the bottom: subgrade, subbase, aggregate base (AB), then asphalt (HMA) or concrete pavement
  • Subgrade prep: proof roll, remove soft spots, compact to the specification
  • Aggregate base: spread in lifts, moisten, grade to cross-slope (often 2%) and compact
  • Asphalt: apply tack coat between layers; place at proper temperature; compaction order is breakdown roller, intermediate roller, finish roller; avoid placing on wet or cold surfaces
  • Drainage: crown or cross-slope, curb and gutter, inlets and underdrains keep water out of the base
  • Pavement markings, striping and signs follow the plans

11.2 Temporary traffic control (CA MUTCD Part 6)

Zones of a work area, in order: advance warning, transition (taper), buffer, activity area, termination.

  • Use proper signs, cones, barricades, arrow boards and flaggers
  • Taper length: speed 45 mph or more, L = W × S; speed 40 mph or less, L = W × S² ÷ 60
  • Example: a 12 ft lane shift at 55 mph needs a 660 ft taper
  • Keep a clear path for the public, pedestrians and ADA access; keep signs clean and covered when not in use

12. Section 8: Safety (18%)

12.1 Personnel and job site safety

  • Injury and Illness Prevention Program (IIPP): every California employer must have one
  • Competent person for excavations, scaffolds and other specific hazards
  • Daily tailgate safety meetings and toolbox talks; incident reporting
  • Heat illness prevention: water, shade, rest and acclimatization for outdoor work; additional requirements at high-heat levels
  • Confined spaces: permit, atmospheric testing, ventilation, attendant and rescue plan (manholes, vaults, tanks)
  • Overhead power lines: keep at least 10 ft away from lines up to 50 kV; more at higher voltage
  • Fall protection, ladders (4:1 rule), scaffolds, and guardrails at the heights in Title 8
  • Equipment safety: operators trained, backup alarms and spotters, seat belts, keep clear of swing radius, secure loads
  • Work zone safety: high-visibility garments, traffic control and separation

12.2 Personal protective equipment

  • Hard hat, safety glasses, hearing protection, gloves, safety footwear and high-visibility vests
  • Respirators when dust, fumes or silica exposure require them (with fit-testing and medical clearance)
  • Fall arrest harnesses and lanyards when required
  • Face shields and fire-resistant clothing for hot work

12.3 Hazardous material safety

  • Hazard communication: safety data sheets (SDS), labels and training
  • Respirable crystalline silica from cutting concrete: wet cutting, vacuum, respirators
  • Asbestos, lead, PCBs and contaminated soil: stop work, notify, test, use certified abatement
  • Fuel and chemical storage: secondary containment, no smoking, fire extinguishers
  • Spill response, proper disposal, and manifests for hazardous waste

13. Calculation practice (answers at the bottom)

  1. A trench is 300 ft long, 3 ft wide and 6 ft deep with vertical sides. How many cubic yards of bank material are excavated?
  2. A 60 ft × 30 ft × 8 in. slab is poured. How many cubic yards of concrete before waste?
  3. A benchmark has elevation 150.00, the backsight is 5.20 and the foresight is 2.80. What is the elevation of the new point?
  4. A 10 ft lane shift is made at 50 mph. What is the taper length?
  5. Soil tests at 0.8 tsf unconfined compressive strength. What soil type is it and what is the maximum simple slope?
  6. A sewer invert falls 3 ft in 600 ft. What is the grade?
  7. 120 ft of #5 bar is needed. What is the weight?
  8. Lab maximum density is 125 pcf and the field test is 118.8 pcf. What is the relative compaction?
  9. A 48,000 BTU/hr air conditioner is how many tons?
  10. A contractor excavates 500 bank yd³ of soil with 20% swell. How many loose yards are hauled?

Answers: (1) 300 × 3 × 6 ÷ 27 = 200 yd³. (2) 60 × 30 × (8 ÷ 12) ÷ 27 = 44.4 yd³. (3) HI = 155.20; point = 155.20 − 2.80 = 152.40. (4) L = 10 × 50 = 500 ft. (5) Type B; maximum slope 1:1. (6) 3 ÷ 600 = 0.5%. (7) 120 × 1.043 = 125.2 lb. (8) 118.8 ÷ 125 = 95%. (9) 4 tons. (10) 500 × 1.20 = 600 loose yd³.

Assumes 8 to 10 hours per week. Adjust to your timeline.

Habits that work

  • Memorize the numbers that show up again and again: soil classes and slopes, the 5 ft / 25 ft / 2 ft excavation rules, 811 and the 24 in. tolerance zone, utility colors, taper formulas, bar sizes.
  • Do volume problems every day. Many questions come down to a clean cubic-yard calculation.
  • Read the question twice. Look for "minimum," "maximum," "first," "not" and "BEST."
  • Answer every question. No penalty for guessing.
  • Connect it to your own jobs. If you manage freeway or utility work, relate each rule to a project you have seen: a trench box, a compaction test, a traffic taper, a pull box set to grade.

15. Where this fits in the licensing process

  1. Meet the experience requirement (four years of journey-level experience within the last ten years in general engineering work; qualifying education can substitute for part of it) and document it.
  2. Submit the CSLB application and fingerprints.
  3. Pass the Law & Business exam and the Class A trade exam.
  4. Post the contractor bond and pay the license fee.

The Law & Business exam is a separate study track; use the CSLB Law & Business study guide for it. If you also want the C-10 Electrical license, see the C-10 study guide in this knowledge base. Note that an applicant can only apply to take one trade exam at a time.

California Class B General Building Exam: Full Study Guide

CSLB General Building (B) exam outline, 2025 code editionsNot yet verified against the current edition

An independent study guide for the CSLB General Building (B) trade examination, built around the official CSLB content outline.

Read this first. This guide is a study aid, not an official CSLB document. Dimensions, spacings and test values are the commonly used ones from the California codes; the code books, plans and the current Cal/OSHA orders govern in practice, so confirm every value in the reference books. The exam is closed-book, so the goal is to know this material, not to look it up. Fees, rules and exam content change; check cslb.ca.gov before you apply.

1. What a Class B license is

The General Building (B) classification is for building or remodeling structures for human occupancy, where the work involves at least two unrelated building trades or crafts (framing or carpentry plus other trades also qualifies). It is the license for house builders, remodelers and commercial building general contractors.

Things to know:

  • A Class B contractor manages the whole job and can hire licensed subcontractors for trades such as electrical, plumbing and HVAC.
  • A Class B license does not by itself let you self-perform every specialty trade; specialty work needs the matching C license (your own or a subcontractor's). Check the CSLB rules on this before bidding.
  • There is a related B-2 Residential Remodeling classification with its own exam.
  • The B exam is broad: 30% of it is "core trades," so you must know a little about many trades.

Question styles in the official guide

The sample questions show the flavor: a code number (the minimum residential hallway width is 36 in.), a geometry calculation (a cylinder 3 ft in diameter and 12 ft long holds about 84.8 ft³), and a cause-and-effect question (a severe foundation crack is most likely caused by inadequate soil preparation). Expect code lookups, math, and "what went wrong or what is correct" questions.

4. Math you must be able to do fast

Core formulas

  • Area of a rectangle = L × W; triangle = ½ × base × height; circle = π × r²
  • Volume of a box = L × W × H; cubic yards = cubic feet ÷ 27
  • Cylinder volume = π × r² × length
  • Pythagorean theorem: c² = a² + b² (used for rafter length, squaring a layout, stair stringers). The 3-4-5 rule squares a corner.
  • Roof pitch is rise per 12 in. of run (a 6/12 pitch rises 6 in. per 12 in.)
  • Rafter length = √(run² + rise²); the roof slope factor for 6/12 is 1.118
  • 1 roofing "square" = 100 ft²
  • Board feet = thickness (in.) × width (in.) × length (ft) ÷ 12
  • Studs: wall length (ft) × 12 ÷ spacing (in.) + 1, plus extras for corners and openings
  • 4 × 8 sheet = 32 ft²
  • 1 ton of cooling = 12,000 BTU/hr
  • Markup vs margin: price = cost × (1 + markup); margin = profit ÷ price

Worked examples

Cylinder. 3 ft diameter, 12 ft long: π × 1.5² × 12. Using π = 3.14: 3.14 × 2.25 × 12 = 84.78 ft³, which matches the official sample.

Rafter length. 6/12 pitch, 12 ft run: rise = 6 ft; √(12² + 6²) = √180 = 13.42 ft (add overhang and ridge adjustment as needed).

Roofing. 30 ft × 40 ft building, 6/12 pitch, gable roof: footprint 1,200 ft² × 1.118 = 1,342 ft² = 13.4 squares; add 10% waste: about 15 squares (3 bundles per square is typical, so about 45 bundles).

Drywall. Room 12 ft × 14 ft with 8 ft walls: walls = 52 ft perimeter × 8 = 416 ft²; ceiling = 168 ft²; total 584 ft² ÷ 32 = 18.25, so 19 sheets before subtracting openings.

Stairs. Total rise 108 in., maximum riser 7 3/4 in.: 108 ÷ 7.75 = 13.9, so 14 risers, each 7.71 in.; 13 treads at 10 in. minimum = 130 in. of run.

Drain slope. 1/4 in. per ft over 40 ft = 10 in. of drop.

Concrete slab. 24 ft × 30 ft × 4 in.: 24 × 30 × (4 ÷ 12) = 240 ft³ ÷ 27 = 8.9 yd³; order with 5-10% waste.

Block wall. A standard 8 × 16 in. block covers about 0.89 ft² (1.125 blocks per ft²). A 40 ft × 6 ft wall = 240 ft² requires about 270 blocks.

Board feet. A 2 × 4 × 8 ft stud = 2 × 4 × 8 ÷ 12 = 5.33 board feet.

Estimating. Materials $4,800 plus 7.25% sales tax = $5,148; labor 40 hr × $55 = $2,200; direct cost $7,348. With 20% markup for overhead and profit: $8,817.60. That 20% markup is a 16.7% profit margin on the price.

5. Section 1: Planning and Estimating (15%)

5.1 Scope of work and code compliance

  • The scope of work states exactly what is included and excluded; write it clearly in contracts
  • Know which code governs: Residential Code (one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses), Building Code (everything else), Electrical, Plumbing and Mechanical Codes, Energy Code (Title 24, Part 6), CALGreen, Fire Code, and local ordinances
  • Know the permit, plan check and inspection process: footing, foundation, framing, rough electrical/plumbing/mechanical, insulation, drywall, and final inspections; no work is covered before inspection

5.2 Design and construction error identification

  • Spot conflicts in plans: dimensions that do not add up, missing details, structural members that cannot be built as drawn, clearance violations, code violations
  • When you find an error or conflict: stop, document, and send a request for information (RFI) to the designer before building
  • Know common construction defects: wrong anchor bolt spacing, missing hold-downs, flashing omitted, unvented plumbing, cut or notched joists beyond limits

5.3 Shop drawings, plans and specifications

  • Plans: site plan, floor plans, elevations, sections, details, schedules (doors, windows, finishes), structural, electrical, plumbing and mechanical sheets
  • Specifications describe materials, quality and methods; shop drawings and submittals show how a fabricator or subcontractor will build an item (trusses, windows, cabinets, steel)
  • Written dimensions control over scaled dimensions; details control over general notes; more specific controls over more general
  • Know architectural scales (1/4 in. = 1 ft is common) and basic symbols

5.4 Field inspection performance

  • Check work against the plans and code before the inspector arrives: measure, level, plumb, square and compare
  • Keep a daily log, photos, and inspection cards; correct deficiencies and reinspect
  • Keep stamped plans and permits on site

5.5 Coordinating the project

  • Sequence trades logically: site and foundation, framing, roofing and weatherproofing, rough mechanical/electrical/plumbing, insulation, drywall, finishes, final
  • Use a critical path schedule: the longest chain of dependent tasks sets the project length; tasks with float can slip without delay
  • Order long-lead items early; coordinate inspections, deliveries and subcontractors; hold meetings and track change orders

5.6 Cost estimation

  • Quantity takeoff, waste factor (5% to 10%), material prices, delivery
  • Labor hours times burdened labor rate; equipment costs
  • Subcontractor quotes, permits, fees, insurance and bonds
  • Overhead and profit; contingency; sales tax on materials
  • Unit pricing, allowances, and change-order pricing

6. Section 2: Framing and Structural Components (20%)

6.1 Subfloor and wall framing

  • Foundation connection: sill plates are pressure-treated or naturally durable (foundation grade redwood) where in contact with concrete; 1/2 in. anchor bolts about 6 ft on center maximum, with a bolt within 12 in. of each end of a plate piece and at least two bolts per piece; embed at least 7 in. into the concrete; plate washers are required in higher seismic areas
  • Wood must be at least 6 in. above exposed earth (and treated where in contact with concrete or soil)
  • Floor system: joists typically 16 in. on center; subfloor of tongue-and-groove plywood or OSB (3/4 in. is common) glued and nailed; blocking or bridging as required; do not notch or bore joists beyond the code limits
  • Walls: studs usually 16 in. on center (24 in. allowed in some cases); double top plate lapped at corners; bottom plate; headers over openings sized by span and load; king studs and jack (trimmer) studs; cripples; corner and wall intersection framing that allows nailing surfaces for drywall
  • Fireblocking in stud walls at the ceiling and at 10 ft vertical intervals, and at soffits, stair stringers and chimney openings, to stop fire spread
  • Nail sizes (penny size): 8d is 2 1/2 in. long, 10d is 3 in., 12d is 3 1/4 in., 16d is 3 1/2 in.; use the nail type and spacing from the nailing schedule

6.2 Roof framing

  • Conventional framing: rafters, ridge board (or ridge beam), ceiling joists acting as ties that resist outward thrust, collar ties, hip and valley rafters, birdsmouth cuts (do not cut more than 1/4 of the depth), rafter ties and blocking
  • Trusses: engineered, set on layout, braced temporarily and permanently as the truss drawings require; never cut or alter a truss without an engineer's approval
  • Roof sheathing: plywood or OSB, staggered, nailed at the schedule, with H-clips as needed; leave gaps at panel edges
  • Overhangs, rakes and fascia; attic ventilation (intake and exhaust) per code

6.3 Seismic hardware requirements and installation

California construction depends on a continuous load path from roof to foundation.

  • Shear walls: wood structural panel sheathing nailed at the required edge spacing (commonly 6, 4, 3 or 2 in. on center at panel edges, 12 in. in the field), with blocking at all panel edges, nailing into framing members of the required size, and nails not overdriven
  • Hold-downs at shear wall ends tie the wall to the foundation or the floor below; install per the manufacturer's instructions and the plans, with the specified bolts or screws
  • Straps, clips and connectors (such as for joists, rafters and posts): use the exact nails or screws the manufacturer specifies; substituting different fasteners reduces capacity
  • Anchor bolts and washers per the schedule; check that bolts are where the plans show before the foundation inspection
  • Cripple walls (short walls under the floor) need bracing, and seismic retrofit adds bolts and plywood to existing houses
  • Tall narrow shear panels are limited by aspect ratio, so a wall segment cannot be too narrow for its height

6.4 Siding and stucco

  • Weather barrier: two layers of Grade D paper (or an approved housewrap) under stucco, lapped like shingles, with flashing at windows, doors and penetrations
  • Stucco (cement plaster): lath fastened over the barrier; three-coat system (scratch, brown, finish) with a total thickness of about 7/8 in. over wood frame; damp-cure between coats per the code
  • Weep screed at the base, kept at least 4 in. above earth and 2 in. above paved surfaces
  • Siding (wood, fiber cement, vinyl): lap and fasten per the manufacturer, flash over openings, keep clearance from the ground and roof, caulk and paint all cut edges

6.5 Decks and stairs

  • Decks: footings below grade and sized for the loads; posts anchored to footings; beams and joists sized by span; ledger attached to the house with bolts or structural screws, not just nails, with flashing so water does not enter the wall; joist hangers with the right nails; guards, at least 36 in. high, with openings that block a 4 in. sphere; decks over 30 in. above grade need guards; stairs on decks follow the same rules as interior stairs; use pressure-treated lumber and corrosion-resistant (hot-dip galvanized or stainless) hardware
  • Stairs (residential):
  • Maximum riser 7 3/4 in., minimum tread 10 in.
  • Minimum width 36 in.; headroom at least 6 ft 8 in.
  • Riser and tread variation limited to 3/8 in. within a flight
  • Handrail 34 to 38 in. high, on at least one side where four or more risers
  • Landings at top and bottom, at least as wide as the stair and 36 in. deep
  • Guards at open sides, 36 in. minimum; balusters spaced so a 4 in. sphere cannot pass
  • Egress: every sleeping room needs an emergency escape and rescue opening: at least 5.7 ft² clear (5.0 ft² at grade), at least 24 in. high and 20 in. wide, with the sill no higher than 44 in. above the floor. The main exit door is at least 32 in. clear width by 78 in. high.
  • Hallways at least 36 in. wide (the sample question). Habitable rooms need at least 7 ft ceiling height.

7. Section 3: Core Trades (30%)

7.1 Plumbing

  • DWV (drain, waste, vent) system: every fixture needs a trap and a vent; drain slope 1/4 in. per ft for pipes 2 1/2 in. and smaller, 1/8 in. per ft for 3 in. and larger; cleanouts at required points
  • Materials: ABS and PVC for drain and vent, copper, PEX and CPVC for water supply; use the right primers, cements and fittings
  • Water supply: pressure should not exceed 80 psi (use a pressure-reducing valve); backflow prevention at hose bibs and irrigation; shutoff valves at fixtures
  • Water heater: temperature and pressure relief (TPR) valve with a discharge pipe to a safe location, seismic strapping (two straps, upper and lower thirds), expansion tank where needed, and in a garage the ignition source raised at least 18 in. above the floor
  • Fixture clearances: toilet at least 15 in. from the centerline to a wall or fixture, with 21 in. clear in front; shower at least 30 in. × 30 in. interior
  • Testing: DWV tested with water (10 ft head) or air; water supply tested at pressure; inspect before covering

7.2 Electrical

Know the basics (see the C-10 guide for depth):

  • Wire size and breaker: #14 = 15 A, #12 = 20 A, #10 = 30 A
  • Kitchen: two small-appliance 20 A circuits; laundry and bathroom circuits are 20 A
  • Receptacles: no point along a wall more than 6 ft from an outlet (12 ft spacing); GFCI in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, outdoors and wet areas; AFCI in most living areas
  • Smoke and carbon monoxide alarms: in each sleeping room, outside each sleeping area and on every level, interconnected in new construction
  • Panel working space: at least 30 in. wide and 36 in. deep, clear; do not put panels in bathrooms or closets
  • Boxes must be secured and accessible; splices only in boxes; grounding and bonding must be continuous
  • Electrical work in a Class B job is done by a licensed C-10 contractor or the properly licensed contractor

7.3 HVAC

  • 1 ton = 12,000 BTU/hr; size equipment by load calculation (Manual J), not rule of thumb
  • Ducts: sealed (mastic or approved tape) and insulated outside conditioned space; Title 24 requires duct leakage testing in many cases
  • Furnaces and water heaters: combustion air, venting, clearances, and in a garage elevated 18 in. above the floor
  • Ventilation: bathroom and kitchen exhaust to the exterior; whole-house ventilation per the Energy Code and CALGreen
  • Condensate drains and safety pans where leakage would cause damage; refrigerant lines are brazed and tested by licensed HVAC contractors

7.4 Concrete

  • Foundations: footings on undisturbed or properly compacted soil, below the required depth and width; reinforcing steel with required cover (about 3 in. against soil); stem walls, slab-on-grade and anchor bolt placement
  • Slab-on-grade: typically 3 1/2 to 4 in. thick on a compacted base with a vapor retarder; control joints cut early
  • Placing: check the mix ticket, do not add water on site, consolidate, screed, float, finish, and cure for at least 7 days (moist or compound)
  • A severe foundation crack is usually caused by poor soil preparation (soft, wet, expansive or uncompacted soil), as the sample question tests
  • Grade the lot so water drains away from the house: at least 5% slope (6 in. in 10 ft) away from the foundation

7.5 Earthwork and surveying

  • Cut and fill, compaction in lifts, keeping fill on firm soil
  • Level and rod: HI = benchmark + backsight; elevation = HI − foresight
  • Excavations 5 ft or deeper need protective systems under Cal/OSHA; keep spoil 2 ft from the edge; call 811 before digging
  • Know expansive clay and its effects on slabs and footings

7.6 Insulation, acoustical and weatherproofing

  • Insulation: R-value measures resistance to heat flow (higher is better); required values vary by climate zone and are set by the Energy Code; install without gaps or compression, with faces and vapor retarders oriented correctly; do not cover recessed lights unless rated for contact
  • Sound: STC (sound transmission class) rates walls; IIC rates impact sound through floors; walls and floors between dwelling units commonly require a minimum rating of 50
  • Weatherproofing: weather-resistive barriers lapped like shingles, flashing at every opening and wall-roof intersection, sealants and backer rod at joints, drainage planes, and caulking that suits the surface
  • Moisture control prevents mold and rot; keep materials dry during construction

7.7 Roofing

  • Layers: sheathing, underlayment, flashing, and roofing; drip edge at eaves and rakes
  • Asphalt shingles: minimum slope 2:12 (with double underlayment below 4:12); four nails per shingle (six in high-wind areas); correct exposure; start course at the eave; shingle offset
  • Tile and shake have their own fastening and underlayment rules; in high fire-hazard areas the roof covering must meet the required fire classification
  • Flashing: valleys, chimneys, walls, vents and skylights; step flashing and counterflashing; seal penetrations
  • Low-slope roofs need proper drainage and a membrane suited to the slope; avoid ponding

8. Section 4: Finish Trades (20%)

8.1 Painting, staining, coating and interior wall covering

  • Surface preparation decides the result: clean, scrape, sand, patch, prime; match the primer to the surface
  • Use low-VOC products as required in California; follow the manufacturer's coverage and recoat times
  • Exterior: caulk joints, back-prime raw wood, avoid painting in direct sun or damp conditions
  • Wall coverings: smooth, prime and size the wall; match the pattern; use the right adhesive

8.2 Tile and stone

  • Substrate: cement board or a mortar bed in wet areas, not regular drywall
  • Waterproofing membrane in showers and tub surrounds; shower floor sloped 1/4 in. per ft to the drain
  • Thinset or mortar with the proper trowel notch; back-butter large tiles; keep grout joints consistent; use movement joints at changes of plane and in large areas
  • Cure before grouting; seal grout and natural stone as required

8.3 Floor covering

  • Check the substrate: flat, dry and clean; test slabs for moisture before installing wood or resilient flooring
  • Acclimate wood flooring; leave an expansion gap at walls (commonly 1/2 to 3/4 in.)
  • Carpet: pad and stretch properly; vinyl and laminate: follow manufacturer's seam and adhesive rules
  • Underlayment for sound or smoothness as required

8.4 Cabinetry and millwork

  • Cabinets: find the high point of the floor, level and plumb, fasten to studs with proper screws, use scribe strips against uneven walls
  • Millwork (trim, casing, base, crown): cope inside corners, miter outside corners, nail to framing, caulk and fill nail holes
  • Woodwork quality grades (economy, custom, premium) come from the North American Architectural Woodwork Standards

8.5 Plaster, drywall and ceilings

  • Drywall: 1/2 in. is standard; 5/8 in. Type X is fire-rated and used for fire-resistive walls and ceilings; install perpendicular to framing where possible, stagger joints, drive screws just below the surface, tape and finish in three coats; levels of finish run from 0 to 5 (Level 4 is common under flat paint; Level 5 under gloss or strong light)
  • Garage separation: the wall and ceiling between a garage and the house need at least 1/2 in. gypsum board (5/8 in. Type X under habitable rooms above); the door must be solid wood at least 1 3/8 in., solid or honeycomb steel, or 20-minute fire-rated, and self-closing
  • Moisture-resistant board around wet areas, but not as a tile backer in a shower
  • Plaster: lath and multi-coat systems; control joints; proper curing
  • Suspended ceilings: grid hangers, main runners and cross tees, level and seismic bracing as required

8.6 Windows, skylights and doors

  • Safety glazing (tempered or laminated) in hazardous locations: glass in doors, glass near doors (within 24 in. of a door edge), glass in or near bathtubs and showers (within 60 in. above the standing surface), large low glass panes near the floor, and stair landings
  • Install windows plumb, square and level; shim at the proper points; flash the sill, jambs and head in the correct order
  • Skylights need curb or deck flashing integrated with the roof
  • Energy performance: U-factor and solar heat gain coefficient (SHGC) are set by the Energy Code
  • Doors: hang plumb, check the swing and clearance, install proper hardware; egress doors must open without keys or special knowledge

8.7 Landscaping

  • Slope grade away from structures; direct downspouts away from foundations
  • Retaining walls need drainage behind them and proper footings; walls over a height limit need engineering and a permit
  • Irrigation: backflow prevention, valves, pipe, heads; California's Model Water Efficient Landscape Ordinance drives efficient design
  • Plant and tree selection, root barriers, and clearances from utilities

9. Section 5: Safety (15%)

9.1 Personnel safety

  • Injury and Illness Prevention Program (IIPP) is mandatory; hold regular safety meetings and keep training records
  • Heat illness prevention outdoors: water, shade, rest and acclimatization
  • Ladders: 4:1 rule (1 ft out for every 4 ft of height), extend 3 ft above the landing, tie off, fiberglass near electricity
  • Scaffolds: competent person, proper bases, guardrails, planking, and access
  • Fall protection: guardrails, safety nets or personal fall arrest at the heights in Title 8; roofing work has its own requirements
  • Tools and equipment: guards in place, GFCI power, proper lockout, trained operators
  • Electrical hazards: stay at least 10 ft from overhead lines up to 50 kV; treat all wires as live
  • Excavations: protective system at 5 ft or more, competent person, safe exit
  • Confined spaces and respiratory hazards: permits, atmospheric testing, ventilation, respirators with fit testing

9.2 Transportation and traffic control

  • Work in or next to a street needs a traffic control plan using CA MUTCD Part 6: advance warning, transition, buffer, activity area and termination zones
  • Taper length: speed 45 mph or more, L = W × S; speed 40 mph or less, L = W × S² ÷ 60
  • Loads must be secured when hauling; know the California Vehicle Code for load limits, covering loads and equipment on public roads
  • Use flaggers, high-visibility clothing, spotters and backup alarms

9.3 Environmental safety

  • Stormwater: keep sediment, concrete wash, paint and fuel out of storm drains; use erosion and sediment BMPs; projects disturbing one acre or more need a SWPPP
  • Hazardous materials: safety data sheets (SDS), labels, proper storage and spill kits
  • Lead and asbestos: older buildings may contain lead paint (pre-1978) and asbestos (many pre-1980s materials); test before disturbing; use lead-safe work practices; hire certified abatement where required
  • Silica dust from cutting concrete, masonry and tile: wet methods, vacuum, respirators
  • Mold, waste and recycling: manage construction waste and recycling per CALGreen and local rules
  • Fire safety: extinguishers, hot-work permits, safe fuel storage

10. Calculation practice (answers at the bottom)

  1. A cylindrical column form is 2 ft in diameter and 10 ft tall. How many cubic feet of concrete does it hold? (Use π = 3.14.)
  2. A rectangular room is 14 ft × 16 ft. How much flooring is needed with 10% waste?
  3. A shed roof has a 9 ft run and a 3 ft rise. How long is the rafter (before overhang)?
  4. A stair has a total rise of 96 in. and a maximum riser height of 7 3/4 in. How many risers and how tall is each?
  5. A wall is 32 ft long with studs at 16 in. on center. How many studs are needed before corners and openings?
  6. A slab is 20 ft × 25 ft × 4 in. thick. How many cubic yards?
  7. A drain runs 30 ft at 1/4 in. per ft. How much total drop?
  8. A 60,000 BTU/hr furnace output is matched with a cooling coil of how many tons if sized equally? (Use 12,000 BTU/hr per ton.)
  9. Materials cost $2,000 and labor cost $1,500. What is the price with a 25% markup on cost?
  10. How many 4 × 8 drywall sheets are needed for 480 ft² of surface?

Answers: (1) 3.14 × 1² × 10 = 31.4 ft³. (2) 14 × 16 = 224 ft² × 1.10 = 246.4 ft². (3) √(9² + 3²) = √90 = 9.49 ft. (4) 96 ÷ 7.75 = 12.4, so 13 risers at 96 ÷ 13 = 7.38 in. each. (5) 32 × 12 ÷ 16 = 24 + 1 = 25 studs. (6) 20 × 25 × (4 ÷ 12) = 166.7 ft³ ÷ 27 = 6.2 yd³. (7) 30 × 0.25 = 7.5 in. (8) 60,000 ÷ 12,000 = 5 tons. (9) (2,000 + 1,500) × 1.25 = $4,375. (10) 480 ÷ 32 = 15 sheets.

11. Eight-week study plan

Assumes 8 to 10 hours per week. Adjust to your timeline.

WeekFocusTasks
1Orientation and mathRead the official CSLB guide; practice areas, volumes, pitch, board feet, markup
2Framing, part 1Foundation connection, floor and wall framing, fireblocking, nails and connectors
3Framing, part 2Roof framing, shear walls and seismic hardware, stucco and siding, decks and stairs
4Core trades, part 1Plumbing, electrical basics, HVAC, code dimensions (egress, hallways, stairs)
5Core trades, part 2Concrete, earthwork, insulation, weatherproofing, roofing
6Finish tradesDrywall, tile, flooring, cabinetry, windows and doors, landscaping
7Planning and SafetyPlans and specs, scheduling, estimating, Cal/OSHA, traffic control, environmental
8Practice and reviewTimed practice; review weak areas; memorize numbers and dimensions

Habits that work

  • Memorize the code dimensions that show up often: hallway 36 in., stair riser 7 3/4 in. and tread 10 in., handrail 34-38 in., guard 36 in., egress openings, anchor bolts every 6 ft, fireblocking at 10 ft, drain slopes.
  • Do math every day. Area, volume, pitch and markup questions are easy points if you are fast.
  • Think "what could go wrong?" The exam rewards knowing why a rule exists (flashing, load path, drainage, cure time).
  • Read the question twice. Look for "minimum," "maximum," "first," "not" and "BEST."
  • Answer every question. No penalty for guessing.
  • Learn the trades you do not do. Most candidates know one or two trades deeply; the exam covers them all.

12. Where this fits in the licensing process

  1. Meet the experience requirement (four years of journey-level experience within the last ten years in building work involving multiple trades; qualifying education can substitute for part of it) and document it.
  2. Submit the CSLB application and fingerprints.
  3. Pass the Law & Business exam and the Class B trade exam.
  4. Post the contractor bond and pay the license fee.

The Law & Business exam is a separate study track; use the CSLB Law & Business study guide for it. See also the Class A and C-10 study guides in this knowledge base. An applicant can only apply to take one trade exam at a time.

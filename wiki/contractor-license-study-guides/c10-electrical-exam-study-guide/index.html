California C-10 Electrical Contractor Exam: Full Study Guide

2025 California Electrical Code (2023 NEC)Not yet verified against the current edition

An independent study guide for the CSLB Electrical (C-10) trade examination, built around the official CSLB content outline and written for people who work in public works and infrastructure.

Read this first. This guide is a study aid, not an official CSLB document. Code section numbers refer to the NEC structure used by the 2025 California Electrical Code (which is based on the 2023 NEC). Always confirm numbers, tables and values in your own code book. The exam is closed-book, so the goal is to know this material, not to look it up. Fees, rules and exam content change; check cslb.ca.gov before you apply.

1. How the exam works

ItemWhat the CSLB says
FormatMultiple choice, four choices, one BEST answer
MathSome calculation questions; a calculator is provided
ReferencesClosed-book; no materials allowed in the exam
GuessingNo penalty, so answer every question
TimePlenty of time is given; read every choice before answering
AuthorsQuestions are written and reviewed by working licensed contractors

The guide shows three question styles. Prepare for all three:

  1. Code and requirement questions. Example topic: bonding and grounding of modules and metal mounting equipment on a roof PV system.
  2. Selection questions. Example topic: what to check when replacing a branch circuit breaker (frame type, AIC rating, ampacity).
  3. Calculation questions. Example topic: total cost with unit price, sales tax and a buy-ten-get-one-free promotion.

Planning/Estimating plus Rough Wiring is about 55% of the exam. Energy production and storage (photovoltaic, wind, generators, batteries, capacitors) appears inside four of the five sections, so do not skip it.

3. Math you must be able to do fast

Core formulas

  • Ohm's law: E = I × R
  • Power: P = E × I (watts); single-phase VA = V × A
  • Three-phase VA = 1.732 × V × A
  • Three-phase current: I = VA ÷ (1.732 × V)
  • Voltage drop, single-phase: VD = (2 × K × I × L) ÷ CM (K = 12.9 for copper, 21.2 for aluminum; L is one-way length in feet; CM is circular mils)
  • Three-phase voltage drop: replace the 2 with 1.732
  • Suggested limits (informational): 3% on a branch circuit or feeder, 5% total to the farthest outlet
  • Percentage: % drop = VD ÷ source voltage × 100

Worked examples

Continuous load, conductor and breaker. A 10 kW, 240 V single-phase heater that runs continuously:

  • I = 10,000 ÷ 240 = 41.7 A
  • Continuous load at 125% = 52.1 A
  • Breaker: next standard size = 60 A
  • Conductor: #8 Cu at 75°C carries 50 A (too small); #6 Cu at 75°C carries 65 A (good)

Voltage drop. 240 V, 40 A load, 150 ft one way, #8 Cu (16,510 CM):

  • VD = (2 × 12.9 × 40 × 150) ÷ 16,510 = 9.38 V, or 3.9% (too high)
  • Try #6 Cu (26,240 CM): VD = 5.90 V, or 2.5% (acceptable)

Three-phase current. A 30 kVA load on 208 V three-phase:

  • I = 30,000 ÷ (1.732 × 208) = 83.3 A

Box fill. A box holds three 12/2 NM cables with ground and one switch:

  • Six insulated conductors = 6
  • One device (yoke) = 2
  • All grounds together = 1
  • Total = 9 allowances × 2.25 in³ (#12) = 20.25 in³ minimum box volume

Estimating. 25 pieces at $4.20 each, 7.25% sales tax: 25 × $4.20 = $105.00; × 1.0725 = $112.61. For a "buy 10, get 1 free" deal, work out how many you pay for first, then add tax.

Motor circuit (10 hp, 230 V, three-phase). From the motor FLC table: 28 A.

  • Conductors at 125% of FLC = 35 A, so #10 Cu at 75°C (35 A)
  • Inverse-time breaker for short-circuit/ground-fault protection, up to 250% = 70 A
  • Overload protection is sized from the nameplate current (typically 115% or 125%), not from the table

Residential service load (standard method). 2,000 sq ft house:

  • General lighting: 3 VA/sq ft × 2,000 = 6,000 VA
  • Two small-appliance circuits: 3,000 VA; laundry circuit: 1,500 VA
  • Subtotal: 10,500 VA. Demand: first 3,000 VA at 100%, the rest at 35% = 3,000 + 2,625 = 5,625 VA
  • Then add appliances with their demand factors (for example, a 12 kW range counts as 8 kW) and use the larger of heating or air conditioning
  • A one-family dwelling service is never less than 100 A

Rule of thumb for conduit fill. One conductor 53%, two conductors 31%, three or more 40% of the conduit's internal area. For example, a 1/2 in. EMT holds nine #12 THHN conductors.

4. Section 1: Planning and Estimating (28%)

4.1 Existing system evaluation

Before adding to a system, find out what is there:

  • Panel and service rating, available bus space, and condition of the main and branch breakers
  • Grounding electrode system and bonding
  • Conductor type, size and insulation condition (look for old cloth-insulated wire, knob-and-tube, or aluminum branch wiring)
  • Known problem equipment: Federal Pacific and Zinsco panels, damaged or overheated terminals
  • Load calculation of the existing service before you add load
  • Available fault current and the AIC (interrupting) rating of existing equipment

4.2 Plans and specifications

  • Read the legend and symbol list, general notes, one-line (single-line) diagrams, riser diagrams, panel and equipment schedules, and details.
  • Specifications win over general assumptions; when plans and specs conflict, the stricter or more specific usually governs, and the contractor asks for clarification.
  • Know scale (for example 1/4 in. = 1 ft), how to measure conduit and wire runs, and how to count fixtures and devices.
  • Know the order of plan documents and the purpose of addenda, RFIs and change orders.

4.3 Electrical calculations

Know how to do all of the following without help:

  • Load calculations (Article 220): general lighting VA per square foot, demand factors, continuous versus noncontinuous load
  • Branch circuit and feeder sizing (Articles 210, 215): 125% for continuous loads; standard breaker sizes in 240.6 (15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100, 110, 125, 150, 175, 200, 225, 250, 300, 350, 400, 450, 500, 600, 700, 800, 1000, 1200 A and up)
  • Conductor ampacity: use Table 310.16, then apply temperature correction and adjustment factors (more than three current-carrying conductors in a raceway)
  • Small conductor limits (240.4(D)): #14 = 15 A, #12 = 20 A, #10 = 30 A

4.5 Material selection

  • Wire: THHN/THWN-2 (building wire in conduit), XHHW-2 (wet, higher heat), UF-B (direct burial feeders), NM-B (dry residential), SE (service entrance), USE-2 (underground service), MC cable, and PV wire for solar source circuits.
  • Raceways: EMT (indoor/outdoor, common), IMC and RMC (physical protection, underground, hazardous), PVC Schedule 40 and 80 (underground; Schedule 80 where subject to physical damage), flexible metal conduit (FMC) and liquidtight (LFMC) for motors and vibration.
  • Breakers and gear: match the voltage, current, frame size, AIC rating and type (standard, GFCI, AFCI, dual-function). A replacement breaker must be listed for the panel.
  • Boxes and fittings: rated for the environment (NEMA 3R, 4, 4X), the use (fixture support, floor box), the volume needed and the wiring method.

4.6 Estimating the job

  • Quantity takeoff, waste factor (typically 5-10% on wire and fittings)
  • Labor units (hours per 100 ft of conduit, per device, per fixture) and labor rate
  • Overhead, profit and contingency; markup versus margin
  • Sales tax on materials, bonds, permits and inspection fees
  • Change orders for extra work, unit pricing

4.7 Energy production and storage planning

Know the vocabulary and sizing logic for:

  • Solar PV: module Voc and Isc, string sizing, temperature-corrected Voc, 125% × 125% (156%) rule for conductor and overcurrent sizing from Isc, inverter output, maximum system voltage
  • Wind and generators: standby versus optional standby systems, transfer equipment, fuel, ventilation, grounding of separately derived systems
  • Batteries and storage: ESS Article 706, battery rooms, ventilation, disconnects, charging controls, working clearances
  • Capacitors: discharge, disconnecting means and overcurrent protection
  • Interconnection: the common "120% rule" for busbar and breaker sizing on a panel fed from both utility and a solar inverter

Worked PV example: a module has Voc = 40 V and a temperature coefficient of -0.30%/°C. At a low temperature of -5°C (30°C below 25°C): 40 × (1 + 0.003 × 30) = 43.6 V per module. Twelve in series = 523 V maximum string voltage. Always check that figure against the inverter and equipment ratings.

5. Section 2: Rough Wiring (27%)

5.1 System layout

  • Locate panels and equipment where working space can be kept clear
  • Plan raceway routes with the fewest bends and pull points
  • Coordinate with other trades (plumbing, HVAC, structure)
  • Follow the plans for the number and type of circuits; use dedicated circuits where required

5.2 Clearance and accessibility (Article 110)

Working space in front of equipment (up to 1,000 V):

  • Depth: 3 ft (Condition 1), 3.5 ft (Condition 2), 4 ft (Condition 3)
  • Width: 30 in. or the width of the equipment, whichever is greater
  • Height: 6.5 ft, or the height of the equipment if taller
  • Dedicated space above the equipment: 6 ft or to the structural ceiling, kept free of piping and foreign systems
  • Panels must have adequate illumination, headroom, and a clear path to the work space
  • Panels may not be installed in bathrooms, closets for clothes, or over steps

5.3 Raceway and panel installation

  • Support and securing: EMT and rigid every 10 ft and within 3 ft of each box; PVC at 3 ft for 1/2-1 in., 5 ft for 1 1/4-2 in., 6 ft for 2 1/2-3 in., 7 ft for 3 1/2-5 in., 8 ft for 6 in.
  • Bends: no more than 360 degrees of bends (four quarter bends) between pull points
  • Burial depth (Table 300.5, general values): direct-buried cable 24 in.; rigid metal conduit 6 in.; PVC conduit 18 in.; under roads, streets and highways 24 in.; GFCI-protected residential 120 V 20 A branch circuits 12 in.
  • Expansion: PVC needs expansion fittings where length change would stress the run
  • Fill: 40% for three or more conductors (see section 3)
  • Pull boxes (314.28): straight pull length at least 8 × the trade size of the largest raceway; angle or U-pull at least 6 × the largest raceway plus the sum of the other raceways on the same side
  • Box fill and box support: boxes must be secured and accessible; fixtures over a certain weight need independent support
  • Panelboards: circuit directory, no more than the allowed number of overcurrent devices, neutral and ground bars separated in subpanels

5.4 Wire and equipment installation

  • Leave at least 6 in. of free conductor in boxes and 3 in. outside the opening
  • Color code: black/red/blue hot, white or gray grounded conductor, green or bare grounding conductor
  • Do not mix AC and DC systems, or different systems, in the same raceway unless the code allows it
  • Use proper pulling lubricant, cable supports in vertical runs, and bushings at conduit ends
  • Splices only in accessible boxes, with listed connectors
  • Receptacles in dwellings: no point along a wall more than 6 ft from a receptacle; GFCI protection in kitchens, bathrooms, garages, outdoors, crawl spaces, basements and other specified locations (210.8); AFCI protection in most dwelling living areas (210.12)

Other grounding facts:

  • Ground rods are 8 ft long (5/8 in. copper-clad steel is common). A single rod that tests above 25 ohms needs a second rod, spaced at least 6 ft away.
  • Bond water piping, gas piping and structural steel as required
  • Separately derived systems (transformers, generators) need a grounding electrode and a system bonding jumper at one point only
  • Neutral and ground are connected only at the service (or the separately derived system), never in downstream panels
  • PV: bond modules, racking and metal parts to the equipment grounding system using listed equipment

5.6 Energy production and storage rough wiring

  • PV source circuits, rapid shutdown requirements (690.12), labeling, and DC disconnecting means
  • Conduit and conductor types listed for the exposure (sunlight, high temperature, wet locations)
  • Battery rooms: working space, ventilation, spill and ground-fault protection
  • Generator feeders, transfer switch wiring and neutral switching
  • Wind systems: tower grounding, lightning protection, disconnects

6. Section 3: Finish Wiring and Trim (11%)

6.1 Device placement and installation

  • Mounting heights and the proper orientation for receptacles (ground up or neutral up is a preference, but follow plans and local rules)
  • Terminate with the right torque; check the manufacturer's listed torque values
  • Tamper-resistant and weather-resistant receptacles where required (dwelling units and wet locations); in-use covers for outdoor receptacles
  • Back-wiring, pigtails and shared neutrals: use pigtails to maintain neutral continuity
  • Switch types: single-pole, three-way, four-way, dimmers, occupancy sensors

6.2 Motor and equipment installation

  • Disconnecting means within sight of the motor and controller (or as allowed by the code)
  • Correct overload device and starter; check rotation and nameplate data
  • Flexible whips with proper bonding; liquidtight connectors in wet locations
  • Equipment connections: HVAC units, water heaters, pumps, appliances

6.3 Labeling

  • Circuit directories in panels that identify each circuit clearly and specifically (408.4)
  • Disconnecting means must be marked to identify the load they control (110.22)
  • Equipment markings for available fault current and arc-flash hazards where required
  • Mark PV, ESS and generator disconnects as the code requires
  • Identify grounded and grounding conductors and phase colors at terminations

6.4 Trims and sealants

  • Fire-stop penetrations through rated walls and floors with listed materials
  • Seal conduits entering buildings from underground to stop moisture and gas
  • Weatherproof outdoor fittings with the right listed covers and gaskets
  • Use listed caulk or sealant around exterior boxes and fixtures

6.5 Energy production and storage finish wiring

  • Final inverter and combiner connections, torque, labels and signage
  • Battery system connections, spacing, and final disconnects
  • Generator controls, automatic transfer switch settings and final labeling

7. Section 4: Startup, Troubleshooting and Maintenance (16%)

7.1 Energizing and testing a new system

  1. Visually inspect the work against the plans
  2. Check terminations and torque
  3. Test continuity and insulation resistance while the circuit is de-energized
  4. Verify polarity and grounding
  5. Energize in order: main, then feeders, then branch circuits
  6. Test each circuit under load; check voltage, current and phase rotation
  7. Document results and complete any required inspection or commissioning forms

7.3 Locating, repairing and replacing

  • Isolate the circuit and work from the source toward the load
  • Use a divide-and-test approach: find the last good point and the first bad point
  • Replace failed parts with equivalent or better listed components
  • After repair, retest, restore labels and record what you changed

7.5 Energy production and storage performance

  • PV: compare actual production with expected, check strings for open or shorted modules, shading and soiling
  • Batteries: check state of charge, temperature, ventilation and connections
  • Generators: exercise on schedule, check fuel, oil and transfer equipment

8. Section 5: Safety (18%)

Safety is rewarded by practical judgment. Know the rule, then know the reason.

8.1 Lockout/tagout

Typical order:

  1. Prepare and identify all energy sources
  2. Notify affected people
  3. Shut down equipment normally
  4. Isolate energy sources (open the disconnect)
  5. Apply your personal lock and tag
  6. Release or restrain stored energy (capacitors, springs, pressure)
  7. Verify de-energization: test-meter-test with a rated voltmeter
  8. Remove locks only by the person who placed them, after the area is clear

Know the Cal/OSHA electrical safety orders for working on de-energized and energized equipment, and know why "live work" requires a written justification and the proper approach distances and PPE.

8.2 Personal protective equipment

  • Safety glasses and hard hat (Class E, rated for electrical work)
  • Hearing protection and hi-visibility clothing where required (traffic work is mandatory)
  • Rubber insulating gloves with leather protectors, tested on schedule, with a field air test before each use
  • Arc-rated clothing and face protection based on the incident energy of the task (NFPA 70E concepts)
  • Insulated tools rated for the voltage
  • Safety footwear; fall protection harnesses and lanyards when working at heights

8.3 Tools and equipment

  • Inspect power tools and extension cords before use; use GFCI protection on construction sites
  • Ladders: keep the 4:1 rule (1 ft out for every 4 ft of height); fiberglass ladders around electrical work
  • Trenching and excavation: know when protective systems are required (5 ft deep or more), competent person requirements, ladders in trenches, and the call-before-you-dig requirement (811 / USA North, DigAlert in Southern California)
  • Aerial lifts and scaffolding: training, inspection and fall protection
  • Cranes, rigging and heavy equipment around overhead lines: keep minimum clearance distances

8.4 Hazardous materials and job site environments

  • Hazard communication: safety data sheets (SDS), labels, training
  • Asbestos, lead, PCBs, mold: do not disturb suspected material; stop work and notify
  • Confined spaces (vaults, manholes): atmospheric testing, ventilation, permit, attendant and rescue plan
  • Hazardous (classified) locations: know Class, Division and Zone ideas and the use of sealed fittings
  • Heat, noise and weather: heat illness prevention is required in California for outdoor work
  • Work zones and traffic control: barricades, signs, cones and flaggers for roadway work
  • Fire safety: extinguisher classes (Class C for electrical fires), hot-work permits

9. Calculation practice (answers at the bottom)

  1. A 15 kW, 240 V single-phase load is continuous. What is the minimum conductor ampacity needed?
  2. What is the voltage drop for a 20 A, 120 V single-phase load, 100 ft one way, on #12 Cu (6,530 CM)? Is it within 3%?
  3. A three-phase 480 V load draws 60 A. How many kVA is it?
  4. A box has two 14/2 cables with ground and one receptacle. What is the minimum fill volume?
  5. A 15 hp, 460 V, three-phase motor has a table FLC of 21 A. What is the minimum conductor ampacity?
  6. How many 3/4 in. couplings are bought if you need 33 and get 1 free for every 10 bought? (Hint: find the purchased quantity first.)
  7. A 100 A breaker is used. What is the minimum copper EGC?
  8. A ground rod tests at 40 ohms. What does the code require?

Answers: (1) 15,000 / 240 = 62.5 A × 1.25 = 78.1 A. (2) VD = 2 × 12.9 × 20 × 100 / 6,530 = 7.90 V, or 6.6%: too high. (3) 1.732 × 480 × 60 / 1,000 = 49.9 kVA. (4) 4 conductors + 2 (device) + 1 (grounds) = 7 allowances × 2.00 in³ (#14) = 14 in³. (5) 21 × 1.25 = 26.25 A. (6) Buy 30 and receive 3 free = 33, so you pay for 30. (7) #8 Cu. (8) Add a second rod at least 6 ft away (25-ohm rule).

10. Eight-week study plan

Assumes 8-10 hours per week. Adjust to your timeline.

4Rough Wiring, part 1Articles 110, 300, 310, 314; raceway fill, support and burial; box and pull-box rules
5Rough Wiring, part 2Article 250 grounding and bonding tables; Articles 210, 220, 225, 230, 240
6Motors, finish, energy systemsArticle 430, 450; finish wiring and labeling; Articles 690, 705, 706
7Troubleshooting and SafetyTest equipment, faults, LOTO, PPE, Cal/OSHA basics, hazmat
8Practice and reviewTimed practice exams; review weak areas; memorize tables

Habits that work

  • Learn the tables cold: 310.16 ampacities, 250.122, 250.66, 240.6 standard sizes, 314.16 box fill, Chapter 9 conduit fill percentages.
  • Do calculation problems every day. The math is where people lose points.
  • Tab your code book by article, and practice finding things fast, even though the exam itself is closed-book.
  • Read the question twice. Look for words like "continuous," "minimum," "maximum," "not" and "BEST."
  • Answer everything. No penalty for guessing.
  • Study with the field in mind. If you work on freeways, link every rule to a job: conduit burial, pull box sizing, grounding of poles and cabinets, fiber and communications raceways, traffic control.

11. Where this fits in the licensing process

  1. Meet the experience requirement (four years of journey-level experience within the last ten years; qualifying education can substitute for part of it) and document it.
  2. Submit the CSLB application and fingerprints.
  3. Pass the Law & Business exam and the C-10 trade exam.
  4. Post the contractor bond and pay the license fee.

The Law & Business exam is a separate study track; use the CSLB Law & Business study guide for it.

Also note that electricians who perform the work for a C-10 contractor must hold state electrician certification through the Department of Industrial Relations (DIR); this is separate from the contractor license.

Ground-penetrating radar

Last verified November 25, 2026

In one sentence: ground-penetrating radar (GPR) sends radio pulses into the concrete and records the echoes from anything with different electrical properties: bars, ducts, voids, moisture and layer boundaries.

How it works

An antenna rolled across the surface sends short pulses of radio energy. Reflections come back from interfaces: steel, plastic ducts, voids, the bottom of a slab, a wet layer. The scan shows depth (from travel time) against distance. A bar crossed at right angles shows as a hyperbola, its peak at the bar's position and depth.

Higher-frequency antennas (around 1.5 to 2.6 GHz) see shallow targets in fine detail; lower frequencies reach deeper with less detail.

Uses

  • Locating reinforcement, tendons and conduits before cutting, coring or anchoring, including multiple layers.
  • Slab and deck thickness.
  • Deck deterioration: on bridge decks, areas where the signal from the top bars is weakened often match corroding, chloride-contaminated concrete. ASTM D6087 covers this use.
  • Voids under slabs and behind tunnel linings.

Calibration

Depth depends on the speed of radar waves in the concrete, which changes with moisture. Calibrate on a known depth (a drilled hole or a bar at a spall), or with the hyperbola fitting the software provides.

Limits

  • Wet or salt-laden concrete absorbs the signal and reduces depth.
  • Dense top reinforcement can hide what's underneath.
  • Interpreting deterioration needs experience and confirmation with other methods: Sounding, chain drag and impact-echo, Half-cell potential and corrosion testing, cores.

Common mistakes

  • Reporting depths without calibrating the wave velocity.
  • Treating attenuation maps as proof of corrosion without other tests.
  • Scanning in one direction only on a two-way mat.

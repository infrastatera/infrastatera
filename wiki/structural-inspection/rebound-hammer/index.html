Rebound hammer

Last verified November 25, 2026

In one sentence: the rebound hammer measures how much a spring-driven mass bounces off the concrete surface, which tells you about surface hardness, and with calibration against cores, a rough idea of strength.

How it works

A spring drives a plunger mass against the concrete. The rebound number is read on a scale or a digital display. Harder, stronger concrete gives a higher rebound. The test is standardized in ASTM C805 (and EN 12504-2 in Europe).

Doing it properly

  1. Choose test areas on smooth, flat, clean concrete, away from edges and visible defects. Grind off laitance or coatings with the abrasive stone.
  2. Hold the hammer perpendicular to the surface. Note the direction (horizontal, up, down), which changes the reading.
  3. Take about 10 to 12 readings per area, at least 25 mm (1 inch) apart, never twice in the same spot.
  4. Discard outliers as the standard describes, and report the median or average.

What affects the reading

  • Surface texture and moisture (wet concrete reads lower).
  • Carbonation: a carbonated surface is harder and reads higher.
  • Aggregate type, age, and whether a bar or a stone sits right below the plunger.
  • Thin or small members that vibrate under the impact.

What it's good for

  • Comparing areas of the same concrete to find weaker zones.
  • Estimating strength only when correlated with cores from the same structure. See Cores and other destructive checks.
  • Combined with Ultrasonic pulse velocity ("SonReb" methods) for a better strength estimate.

Its limits

It only sees the first few centimeters. A reading never replaces a core when strength matters for a structural decision.

Common mistakes

  • Converting rebound numbers to strength with the curve on the hammer, without cores.
  • Testing on rough or wet surfaces.
  • Testing old, carbonated concrete without accounting for the hard surface.

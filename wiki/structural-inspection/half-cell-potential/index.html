Half-cell potential and corrosion testing

Last verified November 25, 2026

In one sentence: a half-cell survey measures the electrical potential of the reinforcement through the concrete; more negative values mean a higher probability that the steel is actively corroding.

How it works

A reference electrode (the half-cell, often copper/copper sulfate) is placed on the wetted concrete surface, and a voltmeter is connected to the reinforcement through a small exposed point. The potential between them is read on a grid. Standardized in ASTM C876.

Reading the results (copper/copper sulfate electrode)

Potential (mV)Probability of active corrosion (per ASTM C876)
More positive than −200Greater than 90% chance of no corrosion
−200 to −350Uncertain
More negative than −350Greater than 90% chance of corrosion

These thresholds are a guide. Look at gradients on the map: a sharp change between neighboring points shows active corrosion better than a single number.

Doing it

  1. Locate bars and check continuity between test points (see Cover meters and rebar locating).
  2. Pre-wet the surface so the reading is stable.
  3. Read on a grid (for example 0.5 to 1 m), and map with contours.

Companion tests

  • Chloride content at several depths, from dust samples or cores: how much salt reached the bar level.
  • Carbonation depth: spray phenolphthalein on a fresh break; uncolored concrete is carbonated.
  • Resistivity: low resistivity means corrosion can proceed faster.
  • Corrosion rate with linear polarization instruments, when the rate matters.

Limits

Coatings, epoxy-coated bars, very dry or saturated concrete and cathodic protection all affect readings.

Common mistakes

  • Testing without checking electrical continuity of the bars.
  • Reading a single potential against the table instead of mapping gradients.
  • Surveying epoxy-coated reinforcement as if it were plain steel.

Steel defects: corrosion, fatigue cracks and connections

Last verified November 25, 2026

In one sentence: in steel structures the inspector's attention goes to where water collects, where stress concentrates and where members connect, because that's where section is lost and cracks start.

Corrosion and section loss

  • Starts where water and debris collect: under leaking joints, on bottom flanges, at bearings, in pockets of trusses and connections.
  • Record the remaining thickness, not just "heavy rust". Clean a spot, measure with calipers or an ultrasonic thickness gauge (see Ultrasonic testing of steel and welds), and map the area.
  • Pack rust between plates can pry them apart and break bolts or rivets.

Fatigue cracks

Repeated loads from traffic make cracks grow from small flaws at stress concentrations:

  • ends of cover plates and attachments welded to flanges;
  • web gaps where cross-frames or diaphragms connect;
  • tack welds, intersecting welds, cut-outs and holes.

Fatigue cracks are tight and hard to see. Clean the area and use Magnetic particle and dye penetrant testing or ultrasonic testing to confirm and size them. A crack found in a nonredundant steel tension member needs immediate attention.

Connections

  • Missing, loose or broken bolts and rivets.
  • Cracked welds.
  • Bearings that are frozen, tipped, walked off their seats or badly corroded.

Distortion

Bent or buckled members, often from vehicle impact, fire or overload. Measure the out-of-straightness and check nearby welds and bolts.

Coatings

The coating system protects the steel. Record coating condition separately (failure, rust breakthrough) because it predicts where section loss will come next.

Common mistakes

  • Writing "rust" without measuring remaining thickness.
  • Missing fatigue cracks under paint or dirt at welded attachment ends.
  • Not checking the bolts around an impact-damaged area.

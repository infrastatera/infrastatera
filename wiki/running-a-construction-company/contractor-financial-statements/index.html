Financial statements a surety and a bank want to see

California, November 2026Last verified November 25, 2026

In one sentence: sureties and banks judge a contractor on three documents, the balance sheet, the income statement and the work-in-progress schedule, and on how much a CPA stands behind them.

Balance sheet

What the company owns and owes on one date. Sureties look hardest at:

  • working capital: current assets minus current liabilities;
  • net worth (equity);
  • receivables that are old or doubtful;
  • debt and who it's owed to.

Income statement

Revenue, cost of work and overhead over a period. The question is whether the company makes money consistently, and whether gross profit holds up as volume grows.

The WIP schedule

The work-in-progress schedule lists every open job:

ColumnMeaning
Contract valueIncluding approved changes
Estimated total costCurrent estimate at completion
Cost to dateSpent so far
Percent completeCost to date / estimated total cost
Earned revenueContract value × percent complete
Billed to dateWhat's been invoiced
Over / under billingBilled minus earned

Overbilling (billed more than earned) means you have the owner's cash in advance; it's a liability, and too much can hide a losing job. Underbilling (earned more than billed) means you've done work you haven't billed, often unsigned change orders or a job going wrong. Sureties read both closely.

Levels of CPA work

  • Compilation: the CPA formats your numbers, no assurance.
  • Review: the CPA does limited checks and gives limited assurance.
  • Audit: full testing and an opinion.

Larger bonding programs usually require a review or audit, prepared by a CPA who knows construction and uses percentage-of-completion accounting.

PM notes

The WIP schedule is only as good as the estimated costs to complete. Update them honestly every quarter. See Job costing: knowing what each job really made.

Common mistakes

  • Giving a surety tax returns instead of construction financial statements.
  • Optimistic cost-to-complete numbers that hide a losing job.
  • A CPA without construction experience.

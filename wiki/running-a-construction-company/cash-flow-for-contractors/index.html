Cash flow on construction jobs

California, November 2026Last verified November 25, 2026

In one sentence: a contractor pays crews weekly and suppliers monthly but gets paid weeks after billing, minus retention, so a growing, profitable company can still run out of cash.

Where the gap comes from

  1. Work done in month 1 is billed at the end of month 1.
  2. The owner pays 30 days or more later; public agencies follow their own cycles.
  3. Retention is held until completion. See Progress payments and retention.
  4. Meanwhile payroll goes out every week and suppliers want 30 days.

On a big new job, the gap grows before the first check arrives.

A 13-week cash forecast

Once a week, update a simple sheet:

  • cash today;
  • expected receipts by week, job by job, with realistic dates;
  • payroll, suppliers, subs, rent, loan payments, taxes by week;
  • the lowest balance in the next 13 weeks.

If the lowest point is too close to zero, act now: speed up billing, talk to the bank, delay a purchase.

Getting paid faster

  • Bill on time, every month, on the owner's format and with all backup.
  • Track each pay application until it's paid; call on day one of lateness.
  • Settle change orders quickly; unsigned extra work is unbilled cash. See Getting paid on change orders and closeout.
  • Use prompt payment rights where the law gives them. See Paying subcontractors and suppliers on time.

Paying out wisely

  • Negotiate supplier terms; pay on time to keep them.
  • Pay subs when you're paid, within the law's limits.
  • Don't fund equipment purchases from operating cash; finance them. See Credit lines, equipment loans and leasing.

PM notes

Cash problems almost never come from one bad month. They come from billing late, change orders unsigned for months and a job that grew faster than the cash to fund it.

Common mistakes

  • Looking at profit instead of cash.
  • Billing late or with missing backup, so the payment cycle restarts.
  • Taking on a large job without a cash forecast for its first 90 days.
